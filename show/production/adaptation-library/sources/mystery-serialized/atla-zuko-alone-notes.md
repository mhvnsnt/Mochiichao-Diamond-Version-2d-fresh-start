# ATLA Book 2 E7 "Zuko Alone" — Structure Notes (quiet two-hander / bottle)

Sample type: (d) Avatar quiet two-hander / bottle episode — here in its strongest form: a *single-protagonist bottle* where almost every scene is a two-hander (protagonist + one other), and the series' main cast does not appear at all (per episode page: the only episode in the franchise with no Team Avatar). Sources: episode page + transcript page, Avatar Wiki (Fandom); Western-homage identification from episode trivia / Avatar Extras via the wiki — see SOURCE.md. Structure only; no transcript saved.

## Cold open — needs stated in the body, not in dialogue
Riding alone through barren country; a bridge nearly collapses under him. Hunger: he reaches for his swords to rob a cooking couple, sees the woman is pregnant, rides on. Thirst, blurred vision — a hooded woman walking away down a dark corridor flashes in his mind (first flashback seed, unexplained) before any town appears. By the time he arrives anywhere, the audience knows: broke, hungry, principled against his own interest, haunted.

## Two-hander mechanics — how two-person scenes sustain a whole episode
The episode is a chain of paired scenes, each pair doing a different job; variety comes from *rotating the second chair*, not from adding cast:
1. **Stranger vs. authority (town square)**: a child throws an egg at corrupt soldiers; the stranger takes the blame with monosyllables (his refusals are one word long). Economy of dialogue = character: a man hiding his identity talks as little as possible. The soldiers steal his feed — injustice witnessed, not explained.
2. **Stranger vs. child (the invitation)**: the child repays the cover with hospitality. Every later two-hander grows from this debt.
3. **Stranger vs. parents (the table / the roof)**: hospitality is re-structured so the proud guest can *earn* it (fix the barn roof, then eat). On the roof, the child's questions are the scene's engine — each personal question the adult deflects, until one (about his scar) makes him hammer his own thumb. **The unanswered question triggers the flashback**, not a narration cue.
4. **Flashback two-handers (mother + child-self)**: pond scene teaches the episode's thesis in miniature — mothers bite back when their babies are threatened (delivered as a joke, with a playful bite). A letter from the front delivers war news *and* two contrasted gifts (a weapon with an inscription for the boy; a doll the sister burns). Sibling scenes establish the sister's cruelty and favouritism in games, not speeches.
5. **Stranger vs. child at night (the swords lesson)**: child steals the swords to practise; caught, he is *taught* instead of punished — one technical correction ("two halves of a single weapon") that doubles as the episode's self-image. Moonlight walk home; the child compares him to the absent older brother — the stranger is being slotted into a family vacancy.
6. **Escalating flashback chain (audience-only knowledge)**: death of the cousin → father petitions grandfather to skip the elder brother in succession → children eavesdrop behind a curtain → sister reports the price (the father's firstborn) → mother wakes the boy at night, gives a parting instruction, walks down the dark corridor from the cold open — flashback and cold-open image finally lock. Next morning: mother gone, grandfather dead, father crowned. *No scene explains what the mother did* — the bottle's central mystery is left as a shaped absence.
7. **Present-day trigger chain**: news that the family's soldier son is captured; the stranger blocks the corrupt leader from striking the father; he gives the child his own childhood dagger (the flashback gift) as a parting present — **the gift causes the crisis**: the child pulls the knife on soldiers and is taken.

## Climax — a duel structured as a western, paid off as rejection
- Town-square showdown, one against four, staged in the street. He fights *without* his defining power for as long as he can (swords only), wins on skill, is knocked flat — and the final flashback (mother's parting words) plays *while he is down*.
- He rises using the forbidden power and states his full name and lineage to the crowd (the identity he has hidden all episode, spoken as a weapon).
- **Anti-button**: victory inverts into loss. The mother steps between him and the child; the child refuses the returned dagger and says he hates him. The family he defended rejects him for *what* he is, regardless of what he did.

## Ending button
No dialogue button. Final flashback fragment (boy finds father silent by the pond; funeral/coronation in white) cuts to present: he rides out of town between two lines of glaring villagers, into a burning sunset, hat back on — the cold-open image (lone rider) repeated with its meaning reversed: at the start he was unknown; now he is known, and still alone.

## Mystery / arc mechanics (quiet-episode rationing)
- Arc payload is entirely *backstory*, rationed in five flashbacks of increasing length and darkness, each triggered by a present-tense sensory/object cue (hammering → pond memory; the dagger → succession memory; being knocked down → the farewell).
- One present-day fact is given to the audience and withheld from every other character until the climax: his identity. The episode's suspense is not "what happened?" but "what will it cost him when this room learns it?"
- The largest arc question (what the mother did that night) is *posed by structure and deliberately not answered* — a multi-season hook carried by a bottle episode with no main cast.

## Homage / reference shapes (shape only)
- Whole-episode shape of the 1953 western *Shane* (per Avatar Extras, via the episode page): mysterious stranger rides in, stays with a farming family, bonds with the son, defends them in a street showdown, rides out at sunset. Structural difference (noted in commentary sampled): the model keeps the stranger's past blank; this episode inverts it — the audience is given the past, the town is not.
- Apple-on-the-head fire shot in flashback — shape of the folk apple-shot feat.
- Lone-rider framing: straw hat as cowboy hat, silhouette-into-sunset final shot.

## Do-not-adapt flags (brief identification only)
- Character/family names and titles, the dagger and its inscription wording, the mother's parting line wording, the turtle-duck pond animal, the stated full-lineage declaration wording, and the title format "<Name> Alone" as used here. Adapt the slots (hidden-identity stranger, gift-causes-crisis, victory-into-rejection), not these identities/wordings.
