# GF S2E2 "Into the Bunker" — Structure Notes (arc inside a standalone)

Sample type: (b) Gravity Falls episode advancing the season arc inside a standalone story. Sources: episode page + transcript page + List of Allusions, Gravity Falls Wiki (Fandom) — see SOURCE.md. Structure only; no transcript saved.

## Cold open (pre-title)
- Two-hander hangout scene (leads watching a bad horror movie, mocking its logic) — establishes the *personal* arc (one lead's unconfessed crush) in the same scene that establishes tone. The confession note is written, then crumpled: personal arc is stated as a goal and immediately deferred.
- A parallel 30-second home-base scene: the owner lies/bribes to cover up damage from the *previous* episode — continuity as a gag, arc housekeeping with no exposition dump.

## Standalone shell (the episode works with no arc knowledge)
A complete, closable unit: follow a new clue → enter a hidden place → survive its traps → meet a monster → trap the monster → leave and seal the place. A viewer needs nothing before or after.

## How the arc is rationed inside it (the key mechanic)
Arc is delivered as **inventory, not exposition** — each beat is a physical object/clue found while doing the standalone task, and each *withholds* its answer:
1. **Entry clue is itself an arc tool**: a hidden-ink page in the journal, read with a black light carried over from the prior episode. The quest is framed in one line of dialogue shape: "find the author, learn the answers to everything."
2. **Layered descent, one revelation per room**: fallout shelter (supplies dated decades into the future → "what disaster was he preparing for?" — question raised, not answered) → recently opened can (someone was here *recently* → author may be alive — hope raised, not confirmed) → security/trap room (journal page solves the trap — the book is a key, not just lore) → surveillance room → hidden lab.
3. **Cross-cut information asymmetry**: the B-pair (sibling + helper), playing with found props in the surveillance room, finds the monster's file note (an experiment number + name) *while* the A-pair is being rescued by the "author." Audience and B-pair know the rescue is false before the A-pair does. Suspense comes from the gap, not from hiding the twist from the audience.
4. **False-answer climax**: the figure claiming to be the arc's missing person is the monster. The tell is a *visual mismatch planted for rewatch*: the impostor's form matches a mascot printed on a found can of beans, and (per episode production notes) lacks the missing person's documented distinguishing feature. The episode's biggest arc "answer" is deliberately a fake — the arc advances by *eliminating* a false author and by what is salvaged, not by meeting him.
5. **Salvage ending, not answer ending**: the group leaves with one recovered object — the missing figure's old laptop, broken, "fixable in a few days." That single object is the entire forward arc payload: a promissory note for a later episode, earned by the standalone climax.
6. **Villain's exit line as arc poison**: before being frozen, the monster taunts that the searcher will never find the author and previews a future image of the hero (screaming, in a pose later reused when the hero is transformed in a later episode). Arc dread is delivered by the *defeated* monster, at zero plot cost.
7. **Post-credits/credits-layer arc**: a decoded credits cryptogram and an in-scene cipher keyword keep the puzzle layer running outside the story (see Do-Not-Adapt).

## Personal arc interlock (why the standalone climax can carry it)
- The sibling *physically locks* the A-pair into a closet to force the confession — contrivance is owned by a character's meddling, not by the plot.
- Confession happens *falsely first*: hero confesses over what he believes is the love interest's body; it is the monster in her shape. Real conversation is therefore still owed.
- **Duplicate-identity test**: two identical love interests; hero must choose. The real one proves herself with a *small private gesture established in an earlier episode* (a zipped-lips gesture), the impostor with a generic flirtatious signal. Identity is proved by shared history, not by a question only she could answer.
- Resolution is a *downgrade, kindly delivered*: she always suspected; age gap; friendship explicitly preserved and a future hangout re-booked on screen — personal arc closed for the season while the location/monster arc stays open.

## Ending buttons
1. Group self-congratulation + comic breakfast plan (ensemble reset).
2. Quiet two-person talk on a fallen log outside (the emotional close — see Zuko Alone notes for the same button used solo).
3. Laptop reveal (arc button).
4. Return to the cold-open movie-night setting, one-line rejection of the channel (bookend gag).

## Homage / reference shapes (shape only)
- Shapeshifter's first true reveal form (spider-like body, long neck, human head) — shape of a famous 1982 shapeshifter-horror film's reveal.
- Bunker layout/entrance — shape of a classic puzzle-adventure game's island structures.
- Fallout-shelter poster and a candy-dispenser spoof brand — background gag brands, set dressing only.

## Do-not-adapt flags (brief identification only)
- The journal, its author, six-finger detail, experiment number/name, and the monster's proper name; the frozen screaming-hero final-form pose as a specific image; the credits cryptograms and keyword; the zipped-lips gesture as *this* pair's identifier; spoof brand names used. Adapt the slots (false-author, salvaged broken device, private-gesture identity test), not these identities.
