# ENVIRONMENT & CAMERA — Visual Language Draft Standard

**Status:** DRAFT for owner review — research only, not yet locked into the Show Bible.
**Date:** 2026-10-27
**Scope:** How five reference shows design backgrounds/environments, move the camera, animate FX, and finish in post — and which of those *techniques* (not their art, characters, or logos) combine into Mochiichao's own visual language.
**Method:** Published sources only — production guides, director/studio interviews, and reputable articles. Sources are cited inline after each claim. No source art, frames, or text are reproduced; everything below is description and analysis for original work.
**Construction-kit note:** Per Jamar's standing rule, what transfers is the *system* — a compositing trick, a camera habit, a colour rule — rebuilt with Mochiichao's own art, cast, and palettes. Nothing identity-bearing from any reference show transfers.

**Mochiichao locations this standard serves:** Garden Gate (threshold / arrivals), the Pond (calm, reflection, night-keeping), the Green (daily life, play, care loop), the Dark Forest (edge of the known garden; dread by stillness — EP01 stinger location).

## 1. POKÉMON (the anime, OLM)

### 1.1 Backgrounds / environments
- **Hand-painted plates, watercolour + gouache.** In the cel era and into early digital, backgrounds were physical paintings — watercolour, gouache and graphite on background board — with a soft, storybook naturalism: layered hills, skies with visible wash, foliage suggested by texture rather than outline. Surviving production backgrounds are documented as exactly this medium and construction. [Source: Heritage Auctions, Pokémon production cel + master background listing, comics.ha.com]
- **The digital transition changed texture, not philosophy.** The series moved from hand-painted cels to digital ink-and-paint (fully digital from August 2002); colours got cleaner and more saturated, but the background stayed a *painted place* the characters travel through, not a graphic field. [Source: "The Evolution of the Pokémon TV Show Animation," eatsip365.com; transition date also in vintageisthenewold.com FAQ summary of production histories]
- **XY era: environments built as reusable 3D spaces.** Director Tetsuo Yajima's team built the gyms in 3D, and — critically for a travel show — built forests and grasslands in 3D "in a versatile way" so the same location family could serve many episodes and camera angles. Backgrounds stopped being one painting per cut and became *places with geometry*. [Source: Tetsuo Yajima interview, *Anime Style* 2016, trans. via truthordeal.neocities.org/translations/yajimainterview]

### 1.2 Camera language
- **Classic era: the stock establishing pan + BANK reuse.** For most of its run the show economised with stock shots and abstract "effect backgrounds" (speed-line / colour fields) behind attacks — the same cel could be spliced over a plain action background without continuity errors. This is a deliberate budget grammar: real places for story, graphic fields for impact. [Source: Yajima interview discussion of prior practice, bulbagarden.net summary + full translation]
- **XY break: "the camera is actually there."** Yajima explicitly stopped using effect backgrounds, redrew backgrounds to fit each scene even when the character cel was stock, and pushed 3D camera work so action feels filmed *inside* the location — things "bursting forward from the background," compositions built for perspective and depth. The synergy of 3D camera + hand-drawn keys is named as XY&Z's signature. [Source: Yajima interview, *Anime Style* 2016, truthordeal translation]
- **Drawing count as camera enabler.** Key animator Masaaki Iwane notes XY allowed roughly 20–30% more drawings per episode than earlier series, which is what makes moving-camera action readable instead of smeared. [Source: Masaaki Iwane interview, *Animage*, trans. Dogasu / dogasu.bulbagarden.net]

### 1.3 FX animation
- **Type-readable attack FX.** Beams, fire, smoke, debris, liquid and wind are drawn as distinct effect families, catalogued cut-by-cut in sakuga archives under Iwane's work — effects carry the *identity of the move*, not generic flashes. [Source: Sakugabooru post tags for Iwane Pokémon cuts, sakugabooru.com]
- **Sparkle / shine as emotional punctuation.** Small star-sparkles and light glints are the show's low-cost "wonder" effect — used for discovery, cleanliness, magic, and cute emphasis rather than power. (Widely documented in episode practice; treated here as an observed convention, not a quoted claim.)
- **Rotation and background animation in hero cuts.** Iwane's signature battle cuts rotate the whole frame and animate the background with the characters — the world moves, not just the fighter. [Source: Sakugabooru #10587 commentary on Iwane's rotating cuts]

### 1.4 Post-production
- **Afureko workflow:** voices recorded after picture (standard Japanese practice Pokémon follows), so picture timing leads and sound conforms — relevant to Mochiichao's "attach audio as each scene is made" rule, which is the inverse order and should be a conscious choice, not a default.
- **Compositing is where the 3D camera lives.** The XY look is a post/layout achievement — 3D spaces matched to 2D keys in both orders (animation-first and 3D-first, chosen per cut). [Source: Yajima interview, truthordeal translation]

**Steal-able system for Mochiichao:** painted-plate naturalism + a small set of *reusable* location builds; stock grammar for travel, real-place grammar for story beats; sparkle as the garden's wonder punctuation.

## 2. DRAGON BALL Z (Toei Animation)

### 2.1 Backgrounds / environments
- **Backgrounds are a specialist painting department, art-directed per episode.** At Toei, layouts go to the art department; the art director — involved from storyboard/layout stage — makes rough art boards, then delegates finished backgrounds to specialist painters in a style "more reminiscent of traditional canvas painting." Background painting is explicitly described as long and difficult compared to cel work. [Source: Kanzenshuu, "Animation Production Guide | The Animation Process," kanzenshuu.com/animation-production/process — a full translation/summary of Toei's production guide]
- **Signature places are graphic, not detailed:** vast empty wastelands, plateaus, alien plains (Namek's green-sky / blue-grass inversion) — huge simple landforms that read at any zoom and leave the frame empty for fighters. The emptiness *is* the design: a stage cleared for impact. (Observed series convention; production-guide structure above is what enables it — one strong plate, reused hard.)
- **Speed-line skies / colour fields** replace backgrounds entirely at peak velocity — the environment deletes itself when speed matters more than place. (Observed convention of the series' battle grammar.)

### 2.2 Camera language
- **Photography as performance.** In Toei's own process language, "photography" (compositing) is where a cut gains pans, zooms, diagonal placement, assigned motion paths, varying acceleration — and, in the digital era, the ability to build 3D space from 2D drawings and backgrounds. The camera move is a *post* decision executed against the timing sheet, not something drawn. [Source: Kanzenshuu production guide, Stage 8 + Modern Digital sections]
- **Zoom-punch-ins and held tension.** DBZ's battle rhythm alternates long held frames (charging, staring, trembling) with sudden zoom/pan snaps into action — the contrast sells both. The held frame is not a budget failure in this grammar; it is the wind-up the punch-in pays off. (Observed convention; the guide's timing-sheet discipline — every frame accounted per cut — is the mechanism.)
- **Rotating battle pans in hero cuts.** Animator Naotoshi Shida's DBZ film work is catalogued with "complex camera movements, detailed background animation, tons of debris" — the rotating, environment-destroying cut is the ceiling version of the show's camera. [Source: Sakugabooru #80591 commentary]

### 2.3 FX animation
- **FX are dedicated cels, made by specialists, with physical media tricks.** Toei's guide lists the FX toolkit directly: drybrushing, airbrushing, charcoal, grease pencils, backlighting, and — at photography — multiple exposures with diffusing screens, filters and gels. Named examples: shadow, fire, smoke, and "in the case of Dragon Ball, energy and ki techniques." [Source: Kanzenshuu production guide, Stage 7]
- **Aura as state, not decoration.** A character's aura (flame-like ki, colour-coded by form) tells the audience power level before a move happens; impact frames — a few frames of inverted/high-contrast art at contact — make hits land harder than the motion alone. The aura-destroying-the-environment cut (Shida) is the doctrine in one shot: FX must *affect the place*. [Source: Sakugabooru #80591; aura/impact grammar as observed series convention]
- **Sound-picture lock:** Toei records dialogue afureko-style and builds music/SFX placement against the finished film's timecode in a dedicated spotting session — effects land *on* frames because someone spotted them there. [Source: Kanzenshuu production guide, Stage 9]

### 2.4 Post-production
- **The composite is re-framed at mastering** (the film master is re-framed/slightly zoomed to hide cel edges) — a reminder that DBZ's "look" includes a final crop pass. [Source: Kanzenshuu production guide, Stage 10]
- **Digital transition (1998–2002) mostly changed post, not drawing:** painting, photography and effects digitised first; the drawings stayed hand-made. Lesson: upgrade the composite before upgrading the art. [Source: Kanzenshuu production guide, Modern Digital Animation]

**Steal-able system for Mochiichao:** empty-stage plates for confrontations; held-frame → punch-in rhythm; FX that visibly disturb the environment (leaves, pond ripples, dust) instead of floating on top of it; a spotting pass where SFX are placed frame-exact against picture.

## 3. JOJO'S BIZARRE ADVENTURE (anime, David Production)

### 3.1 Backgrounds / environments
- **Colour-first environments; realism optional.** Morioh's surrealism is a palette decision: yellow skies, purple hills — taken from Araki's volume-cover colouring, not from nature. Each Part re-skins the world: the environment's colour scheme is part of the arc's identity, not a constant. [Source: Anime News Network feature, "Celebrating The Art and Fashion of JoJo's Bizarre Adventure," 2019, animenewsnetwork.com]
- **Environments as graphic design.** Pattern, flat colour fields, and dramatic silhouettes do the work texture does elsewhere — a location reads by its colour script and shapes. (Synthesis of ANN feature + AV Club production history below.)

### 3.2 Camera language
- **The pose-hold as camera event.** JoJo's camera doesn't move so much as *arrive*: dramatic low angles and freeze-pose holds treat the character's stance as the shot. Director Naokatsu Tsuda's team deliberately preserved the manga's panel compositions — poses are key frames, often held with a palette shift and word effects. [Source: AV Club, "How David Productions Finally Solved the Puzzle of Adaptation," 2022, avclub.com; ANN Tsuda interview, 2015]
- **Perspective bent for drama.** Araki's cavalier perspective — anatomy and space warped for effect — is kept selectively in animation (choice moments rather than constant distortion). [Source: ANN feature, 2019]

### 3.3 FX animation
- **Word effects (onomatopoeia as layout).** Tsuda, in his own words: the manga's written sound effects are "an integral part of the layout," so David Production kept them as *visual* elements — "more of a visual element than a language thing" — including the famous menacing *gogogogo*. The 1990s OVA, which aimed for a cinematic tone, omitted them; the TV series' inclusion is a defining difference. [Source: ANN interview with Naokatsu Tsuda, 2015, animenewsnetwork.com; Sakuga Blog interview with Terumi Nishii, 2019, blog.sakugabooru.com]
- **Menacing aura = typography + colour, not particles.** Dread is rendered by the *gogogogo* glyphs crowding the frame, a palette inversion, and held silence — cheap elements, maximum effect.

### 3.4 Post-production — the headline technique
- **"Special scene coloring" / scene-specific colouring.** Tsuda's named device: because the manga has no fixed colours, the anime refuses a single fixed setting too — colours shift per scene for emotional significance ("scene-specific coloring, so that the 'set color' could still change depending on the specifics of the scene"). AV Club calls the same device "special scene coloring," e.g. Dio's entrance against a vibrant pink full moon. [Source: ANN Tsuda interview, 2015; AV Club, 2022]
- **Music as scene-setting, film-style.** Tsuda wanted film scoring, not variety-show cues: music set to scene/character/emotion and used to *establish period and place* (Part 1's 19th-century England vs Part 2's Art Deco America) — the soundtrack is part of the environment build. [Source: ANN Tsuda interview, 2015]

**Steal-able system for Mochiichao:** one dominant colour script per location/sequence (already in Mochiichao's Persona-5-influenced style stack — JoJo is the *precedent* for doing it mid-scene); palette swap as the cheapest possible "something is wrong" signal; glyph/graphic punctuation instead of expensive FX for dread and comedy — adapted to Mochiichao's subtitle law (rare, important intertitles only).

## 4. THE AMAZING WORLD OF GUMBALL (Cartoon Network Studios Europe / Boulder Media / Studio Soi)

### 4.1 Backgrounds / environments
- **Photographic real places as the world.** Elmore is built from photographs of real locations (Vallejo and the San Francisco Bay Area; some London streets), Photoshopped into a coherent town. Bocquelet's stated aim — influenced by *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* — was cartoon characters living in *our* world, believable enough that kids would ask to holiday there. [Source: Skwigly interview with Ben Bocquelet, 2014, skwigly.co.uk; Kidscreen production profile, kidscreen.com]
- **Mixed-media by design, not accident.** The cast came from Bocquelet's rejected commercial characters in different media, so the pipeline was built to integrate 2D, 3D CGI, puppetry/stop-motion and live-action plates from the start. [Source: Skwigly, 2014; Studio Soi project page, studiosoi.de]

### 4.2 Camera language
- **Sitcom grammar as the baseline.** Family scenes use flat, readable, sitcom-style framing — wide living-room two-shots, simple coverage — so that when the show breaks into action, horror, anime parody or glitch, the *break itself* is the joke/event. Camera discipline in the normal scenes is what makes the abnormal scenes legible. (Observed convention of the series; the mixed-media premise that requires it is documented in Kidscreen + Boulder sources.)
- **Every shot is a composite.** Boulder Media, learning 3D for this show, "pushed what we could achieve in compositing, which played a massive part in making all the different media and styles sit together and feel like they belonged in the same environment." Camera, shadow and light-matching are compositing jobs. [Source: OFFSET interview with Robert Cullen, Boulder Media, iloveoffset.com]

### 4.3 FX animation
- **Style-switch as FX.** Gumball's signature effect is switching the *rendering style* of a character or the whole frame (anime fight, 8-bit, clay, glitch/pixelation in "The Signals") — the effect is a pipeline change, not a drawn effect. [Source: series practice as catalogued in Shapes/Gumball references; glitch episode widely documented]
- **Compositing glue as invisible FX:** matched shadows, rim light and reflections sell every gag — the effect budget is spent on *belonging*, not spectacle. [Source: Boulder Media OFFSET interview]

### 4.4 Post-production
- **Post is the show.** 2D (Flash/Animate, later Toon Boom Harmony), 3D (Maya via Studio Soi, which also took on 2D in-betweening, rendering and compositing from Season 2), photographic plates and Photoshop backgrounds only become one world in the composite. [Source: Studio Soi, studiosoi.de; Kidscreen profile]
- **Production split worth copying:** a small in-house team owns look/compositing; volume work is distributed. The *taste* stays centralised even when labour isn't.

**Steal-able system for Mochiichao:** one "normal" camera grammar for garden daily life, broken deliberately for events; spend composite effort on shadows/contact so puppets, generative clips and plates feel like one garden; reserve style-switch for exactly one gag class (e.g. keeper-device POV) so it stays special.

## 5. CHOWDER (Cartoon Network, created by C. H. Greenblatt)

### 5.1 Backgrounds / environments
- **A food-world built from real architecture references.** Marzipan City's design is based on Moroccan and Indian architecture — domes, arches, patterned surfaces — filtered through a storybook, psychedelic-poster palette where nearly everything is food or food-adjacent. [Source: Chowder production/design notes as summarised in Chowder (TV series), en.wikipedia.org, and series design coverage]
- **Texture is the world-building.** Where Pokémon paints light and DBZ paints emptiness, Chowder paints *surface*: walls, streets and props carry dense decorative pattern, so even a static frame feels rich and edible.

### 5.2 Camera language
- **Stage-like, storyboard-driven frames.** Chowder is a board-driven comedy: frames are composed like a stage — characters enter, play the gag in a readable wide or medium, exit. The camera mostly *stays out of the way* so timing and acting carry the scene; visual richness comes from the art, not the move. (Observed convention of Greenblatt's board-driven production; Greenblatt describes approving boards/art/voices/edit personally across all episodes. [Source: interview with C. H. Greenblatt, kittysneezes.com])

### 5.3 FX animation — the signature device
- **Static pattern-fill ("window" technique).** Greenblatt explains it himself: a pattern is created as a *full-screen image*; the character's clothing/skin area is made a moving "hole" over that fixed pattern, so when the character moves, the pattern does **not** move with them — it stays locked to the screen. Patterns were authored full-screen in the US and executed by the overseas studio (Hong Ying Animation) in digital ink-and-paint. [Source: C. H. Greenblatt interview, kittysneezes.com; technique summary in Chowder (TV series), en.wikipedia.org]
- **Media variety as a gag engine.** The series deliberately rotates media — watercolour and ink-and-paint passages, stop-motion with real food, clay, puppets (including the 30-second puppet credit sequences), even live-action with the voice cast. The switch itself is the joke and the texture. [Source: Chowder (TV series), en.wikipedia.org]

### 5.4 Post-production
- **The pattern-fill is a post/paint-stage decision:** it only works because paint and composite treat the character as a mask over a locked plate — the same layer logic as Gumball's compositing, pointed at comedy instead of realism.
- **Puppet credit sequences** show the show's post mindset: even the credits are a produced segment, not an afterthought.

**Steal-able system for Mochiichao:** pattern-fill is *directly* buildable in our stack — a creature's belly/leaf/wing area masked over a locked pattern plate gives a living-texture gag at near-zero animation cost; Marzipan-style "everything is made of something" translates to the garden: every prop is plant, shell, petal or keeper-tech. Stage-like framing suits puppet scenes and keeps board pitches readable.

## 6. SYNTHESIS — Combinable Qualities for the Mochiichao Universe

The five shows solve five different problems. Mochiichao should take **one solved problem from each**, and no more — stacking all five styles at once is how a show gets no style.

| # | Technique (source) | What it actually is | Cost in our pipeline | When to use it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Painted-plate naturalism + reusable location builds (Pokémon) | One beautiful master plate per location + a small library of angle variants; travel/transition shots reuse them as BANK | Low–medium: paint once, reuse forever | Every episode; the garden must feel like a *place you return to* |
| 2 | Stock grammar vs real-place grammar (Pokémon, classic vs XY) | Abstract colour-field behind a stock action = cheap; the same action in the real location = expensive and meaningful | Low if rationed | Ration "real-place action" to 1–2 hero beats per episode (EP01 already does: Gabu's lunge, Wispkin's reveal) |
| 3 | Empty-stage plate + held frame → punch-in (DBZ) | A cleared, simple background; hold tension on a still; snap zoom/pan at release | Low: a hold is free; the punch-in is a composite move | Confrontations, scares, reveals — anywhere anticipation beats motion |
| 4 | FX must disturb the environment (DBZ) | Effects push leaves, ripple water, bend grass, scatter dust | Medium: one disturbance layer per FX | Any power/effort moment; this is what makes our small cast feel mighty |
| 5 | Scene-specific colouring (JoJo) | The whole scene's palette swaps for emotion/dread; no new art needed | **Lowest cost, highest signal in this entire document** — a grade/LUT pass in post | Mood turns, night, danger, flashback (pairs with the approved pre-mask Wispkin flashback: style shift = time shift) |
| 6 | Word/graphic punctuation (JoJo) | On-screen graphic marks carry menace/comedy | Low | Sparingly, under the subtitle law: graphics ≠ dialogue; reserve for dread (Dark Forest) and one gag class |
| 7 | Sitcom baseline, broken on purpose (Gumball) | Boring-by-design daily framing; the break is the event | Low | Garden daily life vs event scenes; also our puppet scenes (readable staging) |
| 8 | Composite glue budget (Gumball) | Shadows, contact, rim light matched across media (puppet + generative + plate) | Medium, non-negotiable | Every mixed shot; this is the line item that makes AI + puppet co-authoring look like one show |
| 9 | Static pattern-fill (Chowder) | Character area = mask over a screen-locked pattern | Low: a mask + a plate | One or two cast members' markings; food/plant textures on props; *never* on faces |
| 10 | Everything is made of something (Chowder) | World rule: props are plant/shell/petal/keeper-tech, consistently | Free — it's a design rule, not a render cost | All prop/location design reviews |
| 11 | Stage framing for comedy (Chowder) | Wide readable stage, entrances/exits, gag plays in frame | Low | EP01's "things moved on their own" beats — the joke is *in* the frame, not in a cut |
| 12 | Spotting pass (DBZ/Toei) | SFX/music placed frame-exact against locked picture | Low: one pass per scene, already Jamar's preference (audio attached per scene) | Every scene, at scene lock — not at episode end |

### 6.1 Per-location recipes

**Garden Gate** — *threshold.* Pokémon establishing pan (BANK, reused every episode — the audience should know this pan by heart by EP03) + Chowder "made of something" signage/props + one JoJo colour note: the Gate is the garden's warmest palette. Camera: slow push-in on arrivals only; departures get the held wide (DBZ hold). The accepted generated gate plate is the master; build its angle variants now.

**The Pond** — *calm / reflection / night-keeping.* Pokémon watercolour softness + DBZ FX-disturbance rule in miniature (every emotion ripples the water — the pond is Wispkin's face when the mask is still) + JoJo scene-colouring for night: the Pond owns the show's blue-silver night grade. Camera: locked or near-locked; movement here should feel like breathing. Gumball glue matters most at the waterline (reflections/contact).

**The Green** — *daily life.* Gumball sitcom baseline + Chowder stage framing: this is where the care loop plays — feeding, games, keeper work — in wide readable frames with pattern-fill textures on props and one cast member. Camera discipline is strictest here so the other locations' breaks register. Pokémon BANK lives here too (stock play/feed cycles, re-dressed per episode like XY's stock-cel/new-background compromise).

**The Dark Forest** — *the edge; dread by stillness.* JoJo + DBZ, almost nothing else: scene-specific colouring at its most extreme (daylight that doesn't reach in — a grade, not a lighting rig), DBZ's held frame stretched to discomfort, graphic punctuation (the Rule 12 intertitle burn-in is already scripted this way), and a hard rule from EP01's stinger: **the camera does not enter; nothing in the Forest gets a close-up yet.** One leaf falling straight down is the entire FX budget — DBZ's "FX disturbs the environment" inverted: here, the *absence* of disturbance is the disturbance (no birdsong, no ripple, no wind). Chowder/Pokémon warmth is banned from this location's palette until the story earns it.

### 6.2 Camera vocabulary for boards (name these in e-konte)
`ESTAB-PAN` (Pokémon BANK establishing pan) · `PUSH-ARRIVAL` (slow push, Gate only) · `HOLD-TENSION` (DBZ hold, count the seconds on the board) · `PUNCH-IN` (snap zoom at release) · `STAGE-WIDE` (Chowder/Gumball daily frame) · `CAMERA-THERE` (XY-style in-location move — hero beats only, max 2/episode) · `STILL-FOREST` (locked frame, no drift, Dark Forest only).

### 6.3 Post checklist per scene (in order)
1. Composite glue pass (shadows/contact/rim) — Gumball rule.
2. Scene colour pass — one dominant colour per sequence; JoJo swap only at a scripted turn.
3. FX disturbance check — did every effect touch the environment? — DBZ rule.
4. Spotting pass — cries/SFX/music frame-exact — Toei rule, and Jamar's attach-audio-per-scene preference.
5. Pattern-fill check — masks still screen-locked; no drift — Chowder rule.

### 6.4 What NOT to combine
- JoJo palette swaps **and** Gumball style-switches in the same sequence — two reality-breaks cancel out.
- DBZ punch-ins in the Pond or Forest — both locations run on stillness; a punch-in spends their capital.
- Pattern-fill on more than a minority of the frame — Chowder works because the *world* is patterned but faces stay clean and readable.
- Pokémon effect-backgrounds (abstract fields) anywhere except a genuine stock-action gag — XY's lesson was that real places win; our garden is the star.
