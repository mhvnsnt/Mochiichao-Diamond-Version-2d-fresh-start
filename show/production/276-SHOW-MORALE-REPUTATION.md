## The mirror — how the engine reads on screen

- **Reputation precedes a character.** A group's
  standing is staged before the character enters:
  the room's temperature, the shopkeeper's hand
  hovering, the story a stranger tells in the scene
  before. This is #201's device surviving its own
  supersession — #201's qualitative classes were
  always a show-readable view, and under #270 they
  become exactly that: **the classes are how an
  episode *stages* a ladder position** (arrival
  grammar: announced / expected / unannounced /
  dreaded — presentation words for band ranges,
  authored per production, read off #271).
- **Morale is shown in the unseen scenes.** The
  seen/unseen split (#272) is the show's grammar of
  conscience: the audience watches the deed no
  witness saw — the stolen moment played straight,
  without music telling them what to feel — and
  watches the same character, an episode later,
  receive a town's trust they know is misplaced.
  Dramatic irony is the morale meter, staged. A
  character's morale is never narrated and never
  shown as a graphic; it is shown in *what they
  choose when nobody is watching*, and in how their
  own Mochi look at them (#273's turned back is a
  face the camera can hold).
- **Stealing is villain grammar — with one
  exception that is an arc.** The Syndicate steals:
  snag devices on screen are dread objects, and a
  theft set-piece is written under the game's own
  rules (witness ceilings, bond resistance as a
  visible struggle — the Mochi fighting the device
  *is* the bond number, staged). A hero who steals
  is not a beat but an **arc**: the deed writes to
  the character's standing, consequences persist
  across episodes under #82's conservation (a theft
  in episode 3 is still in the record in episode
  12 — reported, wanted, fenced, or confessed, but
  never simply dropped), and the climb back, if it
  comes, runs at #275's pace — a season's worth of
  deeds, not a single apology scene.
- **The ladder is production vocabulary.** Arc
  sheets (#82) carry each principal's two reads —
  personal band (#269) and the per-group standings
  that matter this season (#270, mixed flags and
  hated floors included) — so a writers' room can
  say "he's Ruthless and the town holds him Two-
  Storied Decent" and every department stages the
  same man. Band names on arc sheets are the draft
  words until the owner renames them (#271).

## The long game — why this engine exists in the show

The owner has named the destination (2026-10-27):
**in a later generation of the show — the Gen 3/4
era, when the lead rotates as the reference
franchises rotate theirs — a planned lead is an
original Mochiichao character: a thief anti-hero,
followed as protagonist, whose whole arc runs on
this morale/reputation engine.** He befriends
people to steal their Mochi; the audience travels
with him anyway. Every system in this list is that
character's physics: the unseen deeds the audience
alone witnesses (#272's fourth kind of witness),
the towns that hold two stories about him (#270's
mixed flag), the stolen partners whose turned backs
keep the score (#273), the fences who welcome him
and the doors that close as he climbs (#274/#275),
and a fifty-step ladder (#271) fine-grained enough
for a multi-season climb that can slip.

That is named here as **this system's show-side
purpose** — the engine was built for that arc, and
the Gen 1/2 seasons use it at villain-and-texture
scale so the audience already speaks its language
when the thief's generation arrives. **The arc's
own structure systems — rotation, the double life,
false friendship, part transitions — are List 29's
items (277–286), building separately; this spec
points at them and does not build them.** The
character's name is the owner's open slot; nothing
here invents it, his face, or his story.
