# PART 1 — CHARACTER DESIGN THEORY

## 1.1 Shape language: the three-shape grammar

The foundation vocabulary of character design, as taught from Tom Bancroft's
*Creating Characters with Personality* (2006) and Chris Solarski's *Drawing
Basics and Video Game Art* (2012):

- **Circle** — no sharp corners; reads soft, harmless, complete, calm.
  "Circular shapes in nature [tend] to be soft and harmless and evoke likable
  characters. Many of the most well-known protagonists are designed around
  circular concepts" (Solarski 2012:180, as summarised in published
  shape-language teaching material).
- **Square** — verticals and horizontals; reads strong, stable, dependable,
  sometimes clumsy. "Commonly used for superheroes or the heavy"
  (Bancroft 2006:34).
- **Triangle** — diagonals; the most dynamic and aggressive of the three.
  "Bad guys and villains are often based upon dominant triangular concepts, as
  they appear malicious, sinister and communicate with the most aggression"
  (Bancroft 2006:35). The triangle is the circle's structural opposite.
- **Mixing is the point.** A design is rarely one shape; it is a *ratio*. The
  dominant shape states the first read, subordinate shapes qualify it (a
  circle body with triangular ears = friendly, but alert). Bancroft's ensemble
  rule: shape meaning is **relational** — "by creating a contrast in
  proportions and body shape it is possible to create visual interest while
  also revealing something about the character's personality"
  (Bancroft 2006:132). A shape read in isolation is a guess; a shape read
  against the cast is a statement.

**Mochiichao consequence:** the garden is a circle-dominant world by design
(cute register) — which makes every triangle in it *loud*. Triangle budget is
therefore a real design resource: ears, fins, sprout-tips and the Dark Forest's
verticals spend it. (Enforcement: Design Standard C-2, Part 5a.)

## 1.2 Silhouette theory and the thumbnail process

A design is first a black shape. The silhouette is the only read that survives
distance, small size, backlight, and motion blur — so professional process
tests it first, not last:

- **Thumbnailing** — dozens of small, fast silhouettes before any detail.
  Game Freak's documented process is the industrial version: a brief goes to a
  designer, who produces "quick sketches with few details, which are shown to
  the team" for approval, then iterates through feedback rounds whose gaps
  lengthen as the design converges; "about two or three out of every ten"
  designs drawn are used (James Turner, Game Informer interview, via
  GoNintendo's archive of the piece). The kill rate is the system working.
- **The silhouette carries identity even when parts lie.** Sugimori on
  Hydreigon: the concept (eight-headed Orochi) "can make a creature look
  disgusting," so the final design has one true head — "if you see his
  silhouette, he still has the appearance of possessing many heads"
  (Nintendo Dream interview, translated at Lava Cut Content). The silhouette
  is where the *idea* lives; anatomy serves it, not the reverse.
- **Detail must not break the silhouette.** Digimon designer Kenji Watanabe:
  at Child level "the silhouettes aren't usually very complicated. We add
  detail in the form of belts and other elements **which don't affect the
  silhouette**, that give a sort of Digimon-ish vibe" (Watanabe, interviewed
  by producer Kazumasa Habu, translated at With the Will). Complexity is
  layered *inside* a simple outline, and silhouette complexity is rationed by
  growth stage — a rule Mochiichao adopts for its own stage/variant logic.

## 1.3 Appeal — what it actually is

Appeal is the most misquoted term in the literature because it is not
"cuteness." Thomas & Johnston define it in *The Illusion of Life* (1981) as
"…a quality of charm, pleasing design, simplicity, communication, and
magnetism. Your eye is drawn to the figure that has appeal, and, once there,
it is held while you appreciate what you are seeing" (quoted in Eddie
Pittman's "In Search of Appeal," 2025 — a survey in which working Disney
animators consistently recognise appeal faster than they can define it).
Three operational consequences:

1. Appeal applies to *every* character — a villain's appeal is fascination,
   not charm. A design nobody wants to keep looking at has failed regardless
   of how "correct" it is.
2. Appeal is damaged by "weak drawings, poor design, clumsy shapes" — i.e. it
   is mostly *craft hygiene*, not a mysterious gift.
3. Appeal is a relationship between drawing and viewer: the test is whether
   the eye is drawn and *held*, which is measurable in review (does the eye
   land there first? does it stay?) even if it isn't reducible to a formula.

## 1.4 Gesture, line of action, and design flow

Preston Blair's *Cartoon Animation* (1949; rev. 1994) gives the discipline its
working order: "**An imaginary line extending through the main action of the
figure is the line of action.** Plan your figure and its details to
accentuate this line… The first [thing] to draw when constructing a figure is
the line of action" (Blair, quoted in Cristina Teaching Art's line-of-action
teaching summary). A character design that cannot take a strong line of
action cannot act — so gesture is a *design-stage* property, not an
animation-stage patch:

- The neutral/standing design must already imply its characteristic curves
  (the slouch, the spring, the lean).
- Construction follows the line: Blair builds figures from solid forms hung
  on the gesture, which is why his character *types* (the cute character, the
  screwball, the pugnacious type) are defined as much by posture families as
  by features.
- Design flow: within one figure, shapes should hand the eye along — big
  shape → medium shape → detail — never scatter it. A design whose details
  fight its gesture reads as noise at story distance.

## 1.5 Visual hierarchy inside one character

Every design needs an intended read order *(observed convention, standard
model-sheet practice)*:

- **Primary read** — the silhouette + the one signature feature. Identifiable
  in one second, at thumbnail size.
- **Secondary read** — proportion relationships and the 2–3 large internal
  shapes (belly patch, shell, helmet) that sort the character within its
  species/cast family.
- **Tertiary read** — surface detail: markings, accessories, texture. This is
  the layer that may vary by episode, mood, or variant — *because* it is not
  load-bearing for identity.

Failure mode the hierarchy exists to prevent: tertiary detail promoted to
primary (a character recognised by a prop instead of a shape), because props
can be lost, swapped, or occluded by staging.

## 1.6 Proportion systems and head-count discipline

Designs are specified in **head units** — the character's own head height as
the ruler — because relative proportion is what survives style and scale
changes *(observed convention; Blair's construction chapters are built on
proportion formulas per character type)*. The discipline has three uses:

1. **Type signalling.** Low head-counts (2–3 heads) read young/cute/comic;
   high counts read adult/heroic/menacing. A cast's head-count *spread* is its
   age-and-power map at a glance.
2. **Cast consistency.** A model sheet that states head-count and key
   fractions (eye line at ½ head, etc.) can be drawn by any hand, or rebuilt
   as a puppet, without drift.
3. **Growth logic.** Evolution/growth reads instantly as head-count and ratio
   change on a held silhouette family (see §2.4) — the audience does the math
   unconsciously.

## 1.7 Design-by-function (form follows role)

A character's design must be derivable from their job in the story: the
keeper's silhouette carries tools and reach; the night-watcher's carries
concealment and glow-points; the bruiser's carries mass low to the ground.
Blair's type system is the classical form of this (the pugnacious character
is built from the jaw out because his function is confrontation). The test is
reversibility: shown the design alone, a viewer should be able to guess the
role better than chance.

## 1.8 Ensemble: consistency vs variety

An ensemble works when variety and consistency are assigned to *different
layers* (Bancroft 2006:132, relational contrast, above):

- **Vary:** dominant shape, head-count, height band, accent placement,
  movement family.
- **Hold constant:** ink/line system, eye construction language, detail
  density ceiling, the world's shape-grammar (§1.1).
Pokémon's roster discipline is the documented extreme: Masuda reviews the
whole roster for balance ("This area has a little too much of such-and-such a
color") while final design authority stays with one hand (Sugimori) so the
set coheres (Game Informer process reporting, via ResetEra's translation
thread). One unifying hand + deliberate roster-level contrast checks is the
mechanism — Mochiichao's version is the Design Gate (Part 5c) run at cast
level, not just per-asset.

## 1.9 The design triangle: simplicity / recognisability / expressiveness

Design teaching commonly frames character design as a three-way trade-off
*(synthesis framework used across design education)*:

- **Simplicity** — few shapes, low detail ceiling → cheap to animate,
  readable small, hard to draw off-model.
- **Recognisability** — a silhouette/feature combination owned by no one
  else in the cast (ideally, in the genre).
- **Expressiveness** — the range of readable states the design can perform
  (faces it can make, poses it can take, emotions it can broadcast).

You cannot maximise all three; every design is a chosen compromise, and the
compromise should be *chosen per role*: the mascot buys simplicity +
recognisability and rents expressiveness from body language; the dramatic
lead buys expressiveness and pays for it in rig/sheet discipline. Writing the
chosen compromise on the model sheet is what turns taste into standard.

# PART 2 — CREATURE DESIGN THEORY

## 2.1 Habitat first: form follows ecology

The professional creature-design method, as practised and published by
Terryl Whitlatch (*The Wildlife of Star Wars: A Field Guide*, 2001;
*Science of Creature Design*, 2015), runs like a biologist's workflow:
research real animals → loose shape exploration → anatomical refinement
(muscle/skeleton that could plausibly work) → **characterisation: personality,
habitat preference, ecological role** → final render *in environmental
context* (method as documented in published summaries of her bestiary work).
The order matters: ecology is not decoration added after a cool shape — the
creature's niche (what it eats, what eats it, where it sleeps, how it moves
through its terrain) *generates* the shape:

- A pond creature's body answers water: drag, buoyancy, surface tension at
  garden scale.
- A night creature's features answer darkness: what glows, what hides, what
  hears.
- A burrower, a climber, a floater — each locomotion writes a different
  silhouette family before any "style" is applied.

Game Freak works the same way at roster scale: designers are "always
thinking about what kind of ecosystem they want and how to balance out the
kind of Pokémon found there," grounding expeditions in zoos and aquariums
(Game Informer process reporting, via ResetEra). Sugimori's own origin
account is explicit that the first Capsule Monsters batch failed until the
designs found individual character — expressions, dopey-ness, disgust —
i.e. *behaviour* (2000 interview, translated by Shmupulations, via
Well-Played). **Behaviour is a design input, not an animation afterthought.**

## 2.2 Anatomy plausibility vs cartoon licence

The rule is not "be realistic"; it is "be *internally* plausible"
(Whitlatch method, §2.1). Cartoon licence may break real anatomy freely, but
the design must keep one consistent internal logic — where the weight sits,
what bends, what the creature can and cannot do — because animation will
interrogate that logic in every pose. Watanabe's Digimon rule is the same
principle from the costume side: mecha elements are designed as things a
creature *wears*, "to prevent it from looking too much like a robot" — the
living body underneath stays legible and primary (DigiLab creator interview).
Licence budget rule of thumb *(observed convention)*: a design may spend its
impossibility on **one** headline violation (it floats; it has no visible
eyes; its flame never goes out). Two or more headline violations and the
audience stops modelling the creature as a creature.

## 2.3 Readability classes: silhouette families

Species should be designed as members of **silhouette families** — shared
outline grammars (drop-forms, shell-backs, stalk-and-visor forms, wisp-forms)
within which individuals vary. Families do three jobs *(observed convention,
formalised from the Pokémon/Digimon practice in §1.2/§2.4)*:

1. A new silhouette entering frame is pre-sorted by the audience ("another
   drop-form — water kin") before a single detail registers.
2. Families cap total cast complexity: variety is spent inside families, not
   on unlimited new grammars.
3. Families are the unit of variation rules (§2.4): a family states which
   axes may vary and which are locked.

## 2.4 Designing a SPECIES SYSTEM, not one-offs

The mature creature franchises all design *systems*: a base form plus lawful
transformations of it. The documented axes:

- **Growth/evolution.** Watanabe: silhouette complexity is rationed by stage
  (simple at Child, complexity layered as *non-silhouette* detail first) —
  growth you can read as "same family, more signal" (With the Will/Habu
  interview). Digimon also deliberately values *unexpected* routes: evolution
  lines "are not something we are very conscious of at the design stage… we
  value the unexpected" (Watanabe, via NamuWiki's interview summary) — the
  system law is family coherence, not rigid ladders.
- **Alignment/state axes.** Chao are the published case study in a *neutral
  base form re-expressed by care*: designed as "a relative neutral entity,"
  then raised into Hero or Dark forms with different mannerisms and
  animations, inside an "A-LIFE" artificial-life system with a
  "socialization program" so individuals grow up together and alter each
  other's personalities (Takashi Iizuka, Sonic Team, interviews with
  1UP/IGN, via the Chao reference literature). Design lesson: one base
  creature + a small set of *expression axes* (alignment, type/ability,
  social state) generates a population, not a cast list.
- **Variant/colourway axes** — governed by the sibling colour doc's Lustre
  Law (may re-palette wholesale; may never move accent placement, change eye
  colour family, or touch ink): `show/bible/COLOR-SYSTEM.md` §(b).
- **Age/sex/season axes** *(observed convention)*: lawful when expressed
  through the tertiary layer (§1.5) and proportion shifts — unlawful when
  they touch the primary read.

Sugimori's finishing rule binds the whole system: "**keep the balance**" —
when a design is too cool, add something uncool; too serious, add something
cheerful; memorability lives in the deliberate imperfection (Oshawott's three
freckles, defended against internal requests to remove them) (Nintendo Dream
interview, via NintendoSoup). A species system without a "one wrong note"
rule converges on generic sleekness.

## 2.5 Features as communication instruments

In a show whose creatures do not speak words (Voice Law — pure cries only),
features are not decoration; they are the dialogue system. Design must
therefore assign each species **expression instruments** and document their
ranges on the model sheet:

- **Ears/fins/antennae** — the fastest emotional semaphore (up/flat/swept),
  readable at full-shot distance.
- **Tails** — mood metronomes and, in Kittember's case, a literal signal
  lamp: the tail-tip flame flares before anyone sees what she sensed
  (SHOW-BIBLE cast role).
- **Eyes** — Watanabe's documented redesign is the precedent: beast eyes that
  hid their gaze read as "scary monster"; the anime redrew eyes "looking at
  the viewer, in order to bring out a sense of closeness" (With the
  Will/Habu). Eye *construction* is an intimacy dial, set per species on
  purpose.
- **Substitute instruments** — where a species lacks a standard instrument,
  the design must name its replacement. Wispkin has no visible eyes behind
  the skull mask; expression is assigned to mask tilt, hands, and smoke
  shape (canon, corrected model sheet). An unassigned instrument is an
  expression the show cannot say.

## 2.6 Designing for animation: what a design costs to move

Every design decision is a per-frame invoice *(observed convention, and the
economic engine behind §4.4)*:

- Part count, pattern complexity, and asymmetric detail multiply drawing and
  puppet cost; bilateral symmetry is a genuine production discount.
- Markings that cross joints are the classic cost trap — they must be
  re-registered every frame. Watanabe's belt/zipper detail works precisely
  because it sits *inside* the silhouette on stable body regions.
- A design is not finished when it looks right standing still; it is
  finished when its walk, its signature action, and its extreme expressions
  survive the ROM/proof process (the repo's own rig standard already requires
  the range-of-motion battery — this theory is why).

## 2.7 Small-size and merchandise readability

Pokémon's franchise discipline was hardened by media translation: after the
first generation, "future Pokémon were designed based on merchandising… monsters
that could easily be adapted to different entertainment mediums such as the
anime, trading card game, and physical toys" (Goomba Stomp's summary of the
translated Nintendo Dream material). The transferable tests:

- **32px test** — the silhouette + primary accent survive at icon size
  (already adopted via the sibling Powerpuff pull-in in DONOR-PULLINS).
- **Plush test** — the design survives being sewn: no load-bearing detail
  smaller than a seam, no identity carried by surface print alone.
- **One-colour test** — the design reads as a single-colour stamp/logo mark.

A garden show whose creatures also live in a game (this one, literally)
cannot treat these as marketing afterthoughts; they are design constraints
from the first thumbnail.

# PART 3 — WORLD-BUILDING & ENVIRONMENT DESIGN THEORY

## 3.1 Environmental storytelling: places that narrate

Henry Jenkins' foundational framing ("Game Design as Narrative
Architecture," in *First Person*, MIT Press, 2004): designers "don't simply
tell stories; they design worlds and sculpt spaces," and spatial stories work
four ways — spaces that **evoke** pre-existing associations, spaces that
**stage** enacted events, spaces that **embed** narrative information in
their mise-en-scène, and spaces that resource **emergent** stories.
Theme-park designer Don Carson's companion rule: "the story element is
infused into the physical space a guest walks or rides through" (Carson,
"Environmental Storytelling," Gamasutra, 2000).

For an episodic garden show the embedded mode does the daily work: a
location should be readable as *evidence* — the keeper board with names
added, the worn path, the berry stack half-built. A background that could be
behind any scene in any show has failed the embedded test. EP01's entire
mystery engine is embedded storytelling: things moved, frost trails, a duck
out of place — the garden narrates before any character interprets.

## 3.2 World systems made visible

A world feels real when its **systems** are visible operating, not when its
scenery is detailed *(synthesis of Jenkins/Carson with observed TV practice)*:

- **Economy** — who makes/repairs/trades what; where things are stored.
- **Ecology** — who eats whom, who pollinates what, where water goes.
- **Signage & information** — the world's own labelling (keeper board, gate
  sign, keeper-tech readouts): signage is world-rules written where the
  audience can read them.
- **Wear & maintenance** — the difference between a set and a place is
  evidence of repeated use: mended fences, polished stone, a gate that
  squeaks because it is opened a thousand times.

Hanna-Barbera's cul-de-sac lesson from the donor doc cuts both ways: a world
built once and never redesigned (DONOR-PULLINS, Ed Edd n Eddy) accumulates
exactly this kind of legible wear-continuity — the audience learns the
world's systems by seeing them *persist*.

## 3.3 Layout & composition for backgrounds

The staging discipline from classical animation (staging principle, Thomas &
Johnston 1981: the presentation of an idea "so that it is unmistakably
clear"), applied to the background itself:

- **Focal hierarchy** — every layout names ONE primary focal area; the BG's
  job is to deliver the eye there and keep the rest of the frame in a
  supporting value range *(observed convention)*.
- **Depth staging in three bands** — foreground (framing/occluders, often
  darkest), midground (the acting stage), background (atmosphere, lightest
  or most muted) — with value separation doing the depth work that detail
  cannot (staging/layout teaching practice; cf. the FG/MG/BG layering in
  standard animation staging guides).
- **Rest areas** — a layout must contain low-information zones where the eye
  (and the characters) can rest; a uniformly detailed BG taxes every cut.
- **The BG is designed from the action** — Looney Tunes' pose-first BG
  order (DONOR-PULLINS) is the production form of this rule: layouts serve
  the poses the scene needs, in the locations the show already owns.

## 3.4 Locations as characters

Each recurring location gets what a character gets: an identity sentence, a
silhouette (its skyline/landmark profile), a temperament, and rules it never
breaks *(observed convention in production design; the show's ENVIRONMENT-
AND-CAMERA standard already assigns per-location recipes)*. A location whose
behaviour changes without story cause (the Forest suddenly bright and
welcoming in a filler scene) spends the dread the show banked earlier —
location consistency is a narrative savings account.

## 3.5 Scale language: the two poles

The reference set defines a spectrum the show composes from (full treatment
in `ENVIRONMENT-AND-CAMERA.md` and `DESIGN-PRINCIPLES-DRAFT.md` §5/§3):

- **DBZ pole — vastness:** huge, simple, readable landscapes; scale is stated
  by emptiness and horizon, making small figures monumental.
- **Chowder pole — density:** every surface patterned, filled, worked;
  scale is stated by accumulation and craft.
Mochiichao's garden lives mid-spectrum by location: the Green leans vast/calm
(room for play), the Gate leans dense (the world's systems on display), and
the Dark Forest weaponises *withheld* scale — verticals without visible tops
or bottoms, dread by subtraction (pattern-fill absent; see colour doc §(f)).

## 3.6 Designing a world that serves episodic story

TV economics are a design input, not a compromise (see §4.4): the reusable
stage is the episodic engine. Rules that follow:

- **Few stages, deep dressing.** A small location set, re-dressed by season,
  weather, and story state, beats a new BG per scene — recognition compounds.
- **Story affordances are designed in.** Each location ships with built-in
  episode machinery: the Gate has the board (names, notices, arrivals), the
  Pond has reflections and things that float away, the Green has open space
  for chases and gatherings, the Forest has a boundary that can be approached
  but not casually crossed. A location without affordances is a backdrop; a
  location with them is a cast member (§3.4).
- **BANK logic applies to places too** — establishing shots, transition
  wipes, and signature angles are reusable assets (Space Ghost BANK-first
  law, DONOR-PULLINS), and the world design must *plan* its BANK angles at
  layout stage, not discover them in edit.

## 3.7 Wayfinding and landmarks in a garden world

Audiences build a mental map of a recurring world; design must feed it
*(observed convention from environmental/level design)*:

- **One landmark per location**, visible in its establishing shot, shaped
  unlike every other landmark in the show (the Gate itself; the Pond's
  duck-weathered post; the Green's single great tree; the Forest's tree-line
  wall).
- **Paths are promises.** A visible path tells the audience where scenes can
  go; the Forest's dread partly *is* the absence of a path in.
- **Consistent geography.** Relative positions (Gate → Green → Pond, Forest
  beyond) never silently rearrange; the map is a contract.

## 3.8 The keeper-tech / futuristic-Japan layer

The Persona-influenced layer — UI as a designed object *inside* the world:
keeper devices, signage, and readouts follow the CEDEC 2017 principles
extracted in `show/production/research/persona-5-ui-principles.md` (angular
composition, dominant colour per screen, typography as a graphic actor).
Theory note only, per scope: the layer's design rule is **diegetic-first** —
every interface in frame must be justifiable as an object the keeper owns
and the creatures can react to; non-diegetic UI is reserved for the
subtitle-law moments, which are designed objects too (colour doc §(g)).
The keeper device remains an OPEN ITEM in EP01 (cut 12 HALT) — its design,
when decided, must pass the World Standard's signage rule (W-3) and the
Persona principles doc, in that order.

# PART 4 — CROSS-CUTTING DESIGN PRINCIPLES

## 4.1 Gestalt: how the eye organises everything above

The Gestalt principles (Wertheimer/Koffka/Köhler, 1910s–30s; standard modern
summaries in design literature, e.g. Lazarev Agency's Gestalt explainer;
Fiveable's design references) are the perceptual operating system the rest of
this document runs on:

- **Figure/Ground** — the viewer separates figure from ground before reading
  anything else; designs that fail separation fail everything downstream.
  (This is the perceptual basis of silhouette discipline, §1.2.)
- **Similarity** — like elements group. The cast's shared eye/ink language
  (§1.8) is similarity doing ensemble work; breaking similarity *once* is
  how a frame says "this one is not like the others" (Wispkin in a group
  shot is a controlled similarity-break).
- **Proximity & Common Fate** — grouping by nearness and by shared motion.
  In staging: a creature placed near the keeper reads as *hers*; a group
  moving together reads as one actor (the stakeout's five heads, EP01 cut 13).
- **Continuity** — the eye follows lines and paths; layout leading-lines and
  garden paths (§3.7) are continuity hired as a guide.
- **Closure & Prägnanz** — the mind completes incomplete forms and prefers
  the simplest organisation. Closure is why a few smoke curls read as a
  whole wisp, and why simple designs beat detailed ones at small size.
- **Emergence** — the whole is recognised before the parts; the primary read
  (§1.5) must therefore be a *whole-shape* property, never a detail.

## 4.2 Unity / variety balance

Every level of the production runs the same dial: species within a family,
designs within a cast, locations within a world, shots within an episode.
Unity without variety is wallpaper; variety without unity is noise. The
working test *(observed convention)*: **squint at the set** — if everything
blurs into one note, variety is starved; if nothing relates to anything,
unity is. The dial is set per level in the standards (Part 5), not re-decided
per asset.

## 4.3 Motif and repetition systems

A motif is a shape/pattern/rule repeated with variation until the audience
owns it. The show's motif discipline (full extraction in
`DESIGN-PRINCIPLES-DRAFT.md` §C): each species carries a native motif; each
location carries a pattern family (Chowder kit); the Rule-12 line is a verbal
motif with the same repetition law — repetition is what converts a design
element into a *promise*, and promises are what pay off in stingers and
reveals. Motif law: introduce plainly, repeat neutrally, then spend (the
mask-white seeded in EP01 beat 4 and paid at the reveal is the colour-doc
instance of exactly this law).

## 4.4 Constraints as creativity engines

The documented history is unambiguous that constraints *authored* these
styles: Hanna-Barbera's "planned animation" cut a cartoon from 20–30 thousand
drawings to 2–3 thousand, and the response was a design language — reusable
backgrounds, characters broken into levels so only moving parts moved,
designs with collars/ties masking the head-body split, suggested rather than
rendered environments (Fred Seibert, "Limited Animation… Unlimited
Imagination"; Lambiek Comiclopedia's studio history). The constraint forced
*design* decisions that became the house style. Mochiichao adopts the
principle deliberately:

- The accent-red ration (§1.1 triangle budget; colour doc §(g)) is a
  constraint that creates meaning by scarcity.
- Pure-cries creature voice (Voice Law) is a constraint that forced the
  expression-instrument system (§2.5) — the show's most distinctive design
  asset exists *because* dialogue was forbidden.
- The subtitle law (subtitles only at earned moments) is a constraint that
  makes three lines per episode carry act-break weight.
Rule: when a constraint hurts, the correct response is a design invention,
documented in the bible — not a quiet exception.

## 4.5 Designing for the audience's eye

- **Read order is designed, never hoped for.** First read: the largest,
  highest-contrast, moving element. The storyboard's job is to make that
  element the story point (staging, §3.3).
- **Rest for the eye.** Every sequence needs low-demand frames (holds, wide
  calm shots) placed before high-demand ones; the stinger's stillness lands
  *because* the tag before it is warm and busy. Contrast of demand is the
  pacing of attention itself.
- **One idea per shot** (Thomas & Johnston's staging, §3.3): a shot that
  makes two points makes neither.

## 4.6 Iteration and testing: how studios actually converge

The documented common pipeline across every source in this document:

1. **Brief with function** (Game Freak: concept "for a particular scenario"
   or a specific direction — Turner/Game Informer).
2. **Thumbnails / quick sketches, shown early** — kill rate ~70–80% is
   healthy (Turner: 2–3 of 10 used).
3. **Feedback rounds with lengthening gaps** — cheap opinions early,
   expensive polish late.
4. **Unifying hand** — one authority draws/reviews the final (Sugimori's
   role at Game Freak; Watanabe: "every original draft goes through
   Watanabe's review at least once," NamuWiki interview summary).
5. **Model sheet lock** — proportions, construction, expression instruments
   and their ranges, variant rules (this repo's model-sheet set + rig
   standard).
6. **Proof under motion** — ROM battery / proof frames; design isn't final
   until it moves (repo rig standard).
7. **Field tests** — 32px test, squint test, one-colour test (§2.7), and
   the cast-lineup test: the new design stands in the lineup and must be
   identifiable *and* belong (§1.8).

Any design process that skips 2 (early cheap kills) or 6 (proof under
motion) produces the two classic failures: polished wrong designs, and
beautiful designs that cannot act.

# PART 5 — APPLICATION: THE MOCHIICHAO STANDARDS

Numbering: **C** = character/creature, **W** = world, **G** = gate items.
Colour-specific rules live in `show/bible/COLOR-SYSTEM.md` and pose-specific
rules in `JOJO-POSING-STANDS-DRAFT.md`; both are incorporated by reference.

## 5(a) CHARACTER / CREATURE DESIGN STANDARD

A new species or character design must pass ALL of the following before its
model sheet locks:

- **C-1 Habitat-first brief.** No drawing before a written brief (format
  below): habitat, ecological role, behaviour in one sentence, locomotion,
  expression instruments. (§2.1)
- **C-2 Shape statement.** One dominant shape, max two subordinate; the
  dominant shape's meaning must match the role (§1.1, §1.7). Triangle spend
  must be declared — the garden's default budget is low.
- **C-3 Silhouette pass.** Recognisable in the cast lineup as a flat black
  thumbnail; not confusable with any existing species at thumbnail size.
  (§1.2)
- **C-4 Primary read lock.** One signature feature carries identity; identity
  may not be carried by a prop, a marking smaller than the 32px test, or
  colour alone. (§1.5, §2.7)
- **C-5 Proportion lock.** Head-count and key fractions stated on the sheet;
  head-count must sit in a cast band not already crowded. (§1.6, §1.8)
- **C-6 Design-by-function check.** A viewer shown the design alone can
  guess the role/habitat better than chance; the designer can state the
  design's triangle compromise (§1.9) in one sentence, on the sheet.
- **C-7 One wrong note.** Exactly one deliberate imperfection/asymmetry/
  oddity, chosen and defended Sugimori-style; zero reads generic, two reads
  messy. (§2.4)
- **C-8 Expression instruments assigned.** Every instrument the species uses
  (ears/fins/tail/eyes/smoke/hands…) named on the sheet *with its range*;
  any missing standard instrument has a named substitute. (§2.5)
- **C-9 Species-system axes declared.** The design states its family
  (§2.3) and what may vary along the lawful axes (growth, state, variant
  per the Lustre Law, age/season via tertiary layer only) and what is
  locked. A one-off with no family is rejected. (§2.4)
- **C-10 Animation cost audit.** Part count, joint-crossing markings,
  asymmetries listed with their per-frame cost accepted by production;
  signature action and extreme expressions survive the ROM/proof process
  before lock. (§2.6)
- **C-11 Internal plausibility.** One consistent internal logic (weight,
  bend, capability); at most ONE headline impossibility, named on the
  sheet. (§2.2)
- **C-12 Appeal review.** Shown cold to a reviewer who didn't design it:
  eye lands first in its lineup slot, and the reviewer can say what the
  character *wants*. (§1.3)

**Habitat-first brief format (C-1), five lines, no more:**
`HABITAT: … / NICHE: … / BEHAVIOUR (one sentence): … / LOCOMOTION: … /
INSTRUMENTS: …` — plus one line: `WRONG NOTE (proposed): …`

## 5(b) WORLD DESIGN STANDARD

A new location (or a redesign of one) must pass ALL of the following:

- **W-1 Identity sentence.** One sentence naming what the place *is* and
  its temperament ("The Gate is the garden's front door and noticeboard —
  proud, busy, a little theatrical"). If it can't be written, the place
  isn't designed yet. (§3.4)
- **W-2 Landmark lock.** One landmark, unique silhouette, visible in the
  establishing shot, unlike every other location's landmark. (§3.7)
- **W-3 Systems visible.** At least three world-systems legible in the
  master layout: chosen from economy / ecology / signage / wear — with
  signage following the keeper-tech diegetic rule (§3.8). (§3.2)
- **W-4 Staging layers.** Master layout delivered in FG/MG/BG bands with a
  named focal area and at least one designed rest area; value separation
  between bands verified in greyscale. (§3.3)
- **W-5 Story affordances.** At least three built-in episode mechanisms the
  place affords (things that can be lost/found/flooded/hidden/announced
  there), listed on the location sheet. (§3.6)
- **W-6 Geography contract.** The location's position relative to existing
  locations is fixed on the garden map; paths in/out drawn; any place the
  camera may not go is stated as a rule, not a habit. (§3.7)
- **W-7 Scale position declared.** Where the location sits on the
  vast↔dense spectrum (§3.5) and what states its scale (emptiness /
  accumulation / withholding).
- **W-8 BANK plan.** Establishing shot, two reusable angles, and one
  signature transition are planned at layout stage. (§3.6)
- **W-9 Embedded evidence.** The master layout contains at least two pieces
  of embedded narrative evidence (traces of past events/habits) that a
  script can point a mystery at. (§3.1)
- **W-10 Dressing states.** Day / dusk / night (and any story-state variant)
  specified at design time — as *states of one design*, not new designs.
  Colour grading per the sibling colour doc's location scripts.

## 5(d) WORKED AUDITS — pilot six + EP01 locations

Verdicts grounded in the repo's actual art and sheets as built. **PASS** =
meets the standard now; **FIX** = named gap, owner of the fix named by doc.

### Pilot cast

- **Mochii** (blue water-drop slime) — **PASS.** Near-pure circle/drop
  dominant shape: the mascot read is the shape grammar working as designed
  (C-2). Primary read survives every test we have (C-3/C-4). Triangle
  compromise stated by its existence: simplicity + recognisability bought,
  expressiveness rented from whole-body squash (C-6). *Watch item:* the
  deep-blue lustre colourway must stay inside the Lustre Law (G-3).
- **Dorowani** (blue bipedal; red fin cheeks; dorsal fin; tan shell) —
  **PASS, one production FIX.** Fin cheeks are a textbook primary read —
  silhouette signature + accent placement in one feature (C-4), and the
  accent-red ration is spent exactly where identity lives (colour doc §(g)).
  FIX (already logged in its rig build): the master art's thin white
  fin-streak elements sweep across the face on head rotation — a tertiary
  detail interfering with the primary read in motion; paint out or split as
  separate symbols at assembly (C-10 cost audit doing its job).
- **Gabu** (teal, turtle-like; long arms; big laugh) — **PASS with a
  silhouette watch.** Square/low-mass bruiser read is correct for the role
  (C-2/C-6), and long arms give him a gesture family nobody else owns
  (§1.4). Watch: "turtle-like" is a crowded genre silhouette — his identity
  must keep riding on the arm proportions + shell profile in the lineup
  test (G-1), because generic-turtle drift is precisely the failure this
  standard exists to catch early.
- **Kittember** (black cat; golden eyes; flame on tail tip) — **PASS
  (canon-corrected).** The strongest accent discipline in the cast: one
  warm signal on a black body, and the accent is a working instrument
  (C-8) — the tail flame is her dialogue. The audit's one historical fail
  is instructive: art/specs that treated the flame as a floating companion
  orb violated C-4/C-8 (identity + instrument misplaced). Corrected across
  sheet, specs, and EP01 docs: **flame is tail-tip-attached, always.**
- **Sproutle** (mint; green helmet; blue visor) — **FIX (documentation
  class).** The design itself passes shape/proportion checks — helmet +
  visor give a stalk-and-visor family read (§2.3) nobody else uses. The
  fail was reference discipline: portrait colourway and lustre sprite
  disagree, and two references means no reference. Verdict stands as
  already ruled: **the portrait is the show reference**; the sprite is a
  game variant under the Lustre Law. Sheet must carry that sentence (it
  does) and any future asset generated from the sprite colourway fails
  G-3 automatically.
- **Wispkin** (dark wisp over smoke; skull mask; small black hands) —
  **PASS (canon-corrected), highest design value in the cast.** The mask
  *is* the primary read (C-4) and the show's clearest §2.2 case: one
  headline impossibility (a floating wisp wearing a mask it must hold),
  spent once, legible forever. Expression instruments were the trap —
  no visible eyes — and the design answers it correctly: mask tilt,
  hands, smoke shape are the assigned substitutes (C-8). Original sheet
  documented the pre-mask sprite look as the design; corrected to the
  canon masked cutout. The pre-mask form survives lawfully as a
  flashback-only state (a §2.4 state-axis variant with an owner ruling
  attached — exactly how state axes are supposed to work).

### EP01 locations

- **Garden Gate** — **PASS.** Identity sentence writes itself (W-1:
  threshold + noticeboard), landmark is the Gate itself (W-2), systems
  visible in the accepted plate: signage (keeper board), wear (path,
  plantings), economy (the keeper's post) (W-3). Affordances already
  used by EP01: arrivals, announcements, names added to the board
  (W-5). This is the location the others are measured against.
- **The Pond** — **PASS with one FIX.** Staging and states are strong
  (dusk variant earns the stakeout; reflections are a built-in
  affordance, W-5/W-10). FIX: at thumbnail distance the Pond's landmark
  is weaker than the Gate's — it needs one signature vertical (the
  duck-weathered post / a keeper bell-buoy) locked on its location
  sheet (W-2) before its master BG is built. (Picture side is already
  honestly flagged pending in the Scene 2 gate report.)
- **The Green** — **FIX.** Temperament (calm open stage, §3.5 vast pole)
  is right and EP01 uses it correctly as the play/chase floor — but its
  identity sentence is currently "the open bit," which is a function,
  not an identity (W-1 fail), and its affordances are generic. Fix: give
  the Green one signature system — the berry-stack / training-post /
  gathering-stone, one of them, made canonical on the location sheet —
  and let episodes hang on it. Cheapest fix on this list; do it before
  EP02 boards, not before EP01 picture.
- **The Dark Forest** — **PASS as design, MAKE as asset.** The design is
  the most disciplined in the show: identity = withheld scale and absent
  systems (W-7 by negation — no signage, no wear, no path: W-3/W-6
  satisfied *by stated absence*, which is the dread); pattern-fill
  absence (colour doc §(f)) and the camera-never-enters rule are its
  laws. The gap is purely execution: the daylit-horror BG is a MAKE
  item (storyboard cut 25), and what stopped moving between the trunks
  remains an OPEN ITEM the design deliberately does not answer (G-12).
