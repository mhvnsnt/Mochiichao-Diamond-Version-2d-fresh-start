# PART 1 — HOW THE SOURCES USE COLOUR

## 1. Pokémon (games + anime)

### 1.1 Silhouette first, then ONE dominant colour + accents

Ken Sugimori, Game Freak's lead character designer/art director, is documented
prioritising "readability and personality… each creature has a distinct silhouette"
and designs simple enough that "a child [can] draw" them yet instantly recognisable
(Shapes.inc author guide summarising Sugimori's published interviews). Colour rides
on top of that silhouette discipline: the franchise literally classifies every
species by **one** official colour — the Pokédex colour list (red, blue, yellow,
green, black, brown, purple, gray, white, pink — orange is not a category) assigns
each Pokémon "the one that predominates over its body," official since
*Ruby/Sapphire*'s Pokédex sorting (Pokémon Wiki, "List of Pokémon by color,"
documenting the game data). One species = one headline colour. Accents (belly,
markings, eyes) are a second, smaller read layered under the dominant.

**Lesson shape:** dominant colour is a *classification system*, not decoration.
The audience files the creature under its dominant before they register detail.

### 1.2 Type colour-coding as an information system

Sugimori "established the elemental color-coding system that helps players
intuitively understand a Pokémon's type at a glance" (Shapes.inc, above). Across
games, TCG, and UI, type iconography holds a stable colour association (Fire reads
red/orange, Water blue, Grass green, Electric yellow, Psychic pink/magenta, Ghost
deep purple, etc. — maintained consistently enough that Bulbapedia's colour-template
system documents light/base/dark values for move categories pulled from official
game UI, e.g. *Pokémon Masters EX* icons: Bulbapedia, "Help:Color templates").
The body colour of a species usually *agrees* with its type colour, so the creature
itself is the UI: you know what it does before any text appears.

### 1.3 Sugimori's "keep the balance" — the memorable flaw

Sugimori's finishing doctrine, from a translated interview (Nintendo Dream, via
Siliconera; reported by Nintendo Life, 28 Jul 2018, and Nintendo Soup): "The
technique I often use when finishing up designs is to 'keep the balance.' I might
try adding something uncool to a Pokémon that is too cool, or something cheerful to
a Pokémon that is too serious… if it looks too cool then it takes away from what
makes it memorable." His example: Oshawott's three freckles — removing them makes it
cuter but less memorable; he insisted they stay. Colour implication: a species
palette is allowed — required — one *off-note*: an accent that breaks pure harmony
so the design sticks. (Part 2 turns this into the "one wrong note" rule.)

### 1.4 Shiny / variant colourways — hand-picked, not algorithmic

- Shiny Pokémon (introduced *Gold/Silver*, 1999) have "different color palettes from
  the standard color of their species, with each type of Pokémon having only one
  predetermined shiny palette" (Wikipedia, "Shiny Pokémon"). One variant per
  species, fixed for everyone — a variant is a *species fact*, not per-individual.
- The persistent fan rumour that early shinies were an automatic palette swap is
  debunked in Bulbapedia's design notes: "While it is often rumored that early Shiny
  colorations were determined algorithmically, all Pokémon's Shiny colorations are
  individually defined in the game data, and Pokémon with similar palettes have
  distinct Shiny colorations even in Generation II." Every shiny was *chosen*.
- Designer James Turner "stated he was only able to design the Shiny palettes of his
  designs starting from the 2016 games *Pokémon Sun* and *Moon*" (Wikipedia/
  Bulbapedia, citing Turner) — i.e., from Gen VII the species' own designer owns the
  variant too. The variant is part of the design deliverable, not an afterthought.
- **What a shiny may change varies wildly and deliberately:** some are "only a few
  shades darker or lighter" (Glaceon, Pichu); others are dramatic re-palettes
  (Caterpie, Smeargle) (Bulbapedia). Evolution lines are NOT guaranteed consistent
  shinies: standard Ponyta and Rapidash both have orange flames, but shiny Ponyta's
  flames are blue while shiny Rapidash's are gray (Bulbapedia). The constant across
  all of it: **the silhouette and the design's identifying marks never change** —
  only the palette assignment does. Rarity itself is the signal (very low encounter
  odds), amplified by a sparkle/star animation on entry in the games — the *event*
  announces the variant before the eye parses the new colours.
- Note for our purposes: "Shiny Pokémon do not have their own [Pokédex] colors"
  (Pokémon Wiki, above) — a variant does not reclassify the species. The dominant-
  colour filing stays the base form's.

### 1.5 Regional / variant forms — palette + silhouette can both move, but identity holds

Regional forms (e.g. Kantonian Rattata purple/quadruped vs Alolan Rattata
black/bipedal, also gaining the Dark type) change colour, posture, and even type —
but keep the species' core identifying structure (face, tail, proportions family)
so the lineage reads instantly (examples documented in Bulbapedia/Wikipedia species
coverage). Rule of thumb extractable from the practice: a *form* may re-palette AND
re-pose; a *colourway* (shiny) may only re-palette.

### 1.6 Anime colour staging

The TV anime treats backgrounds as graded emotional staging rather than neutral
space: the same location is re-graded across day/sunset/night versions, and battle
and "world" scenes push saturation up while quiet scenes sit in softer, lower-
contrast ranges *(observed convention across the OLM-produced series)*. Sugimori-era
(1990s) official art was watercolour with muted, slightly chalky saturation; modern
franchise art is digitally coloured, cleaner and higher-chroma — a shift visible
comparing Sugimori's early guidebook art with current official art and noted in
career retrospectives of his work (Dogasu's Backpack, "Sugimori Ken's Works"
portfolio review, documenting his move from pen-and-watercolour to scanned line +
digital colour). Takeaway: **era = saturation policy**. A production must pick its
saturation era and hold it, or the cast looks like it came from different decades.

## 2. Digimon

### 2.1 Attribute colour-coding (Vaccine / Data / Virus / Free)

Digimon's attribute triangle (Vaccine beats Virus, Virus beats Data, Data beats
Vaccine; Free is neutral) is displayed to players by **shape icon + colour** in
products: in *Digimon Story: Cyber Sleuth*, attributes are shown by symbol
(Vaccine = teardrop, Virus = spiked ball, Data = cube, Free = dash) and the nine
*elements* are shown by colour (Fire red, Plant dark green, Water dark blue, Earth
brown, Electric yellow, Wind light green, Light white, Dark purple, Neutral gray) —
documented in player guides to Cyber Sleuth (GameFAQs guide; Steam Community
guide). The *Pendulum Color* product line's reference documentation displays
attributes with colour families: "Red/Orange/Yellow/Pink for Vaccine, Greens for
Data, Violets/Brown for Virus and Blues/Gray for Free" (Humulos, Digimon Pendulum
Color evolution guide). Note the discipline: attribute meaning is carried by a
*redundant* code (shape AND colour), so it survives small screens and colour-blind
viewing — colour is never the only channel.

### 2.2 Colourway = attribute signal: the MetalGreymon case

The cleanest documented colourway-as-information example in the franchise: "This
**yellow-colored** MetalGreymon with **Vaccine** Attribute is distinguished from
the **blue-colored** MetalGreymon with **Virus** Attribute" (Wikimon, "Metal
Greymon (Virus)"). Same species, same design — the palette swap alone tells you
which moral/mechanical faction it belongs to. This is shiny-logic put to narrative
work: identical silhouette, palette carries allegiance.

### 2.3 Partner-pair colour relationships

Across the anime and products, child + partner are colour-staged as a pair: the
human's signature colour (clothing, crest glow, Digivice activation colour) rhymes
with the partner's dominant or accent, so a two-shot reads as one unit. The
Digivice itself dramatises this: in the Adventure continuity, Digivices "only
become colored when they enter an 'activated' state, when the partner Digimon Super
Evolves," and special evolutions push the device into the pair's crest colour or
white-out states (Wikimon, "Digivice," documenting *Last Evolution Kizuna*).
Colour is the bond meter: the human's prop literally lights up in the pair's
colour when the relationship does something.

### 2.4 Evolution-stage palette escalation

Evolution lines escalate palette with stage: early stages sit in one or two soft
colours; Perfect/Ultimate stages add metallics (chrome/gold/silver armour), higher
saturation, and more accent colours, so *power reads as palette complexity +
contrast*, not just size. The Agumon line is the canonical teaching example
(yellow child → orange dinosaur → chrome-and-orange cyborg → gold-accented knight),
traceable through Wikimon's species/line documentation. Dark evolutions invert the
escalation: same complexity, desaturated and shifted cold — palette temperature
tells you the evolution went *wrong* before the story does *(observed convention
in Adventure/Tamers dark-evolution episodes)*.

### 2.5 Digital World vs real world colour treatment

The anime's standing convention: the Digital World is graded *off* — skies in
unnatural hues, harder saturation, flatter light — while the real world sits in
naturalistic, lower-saturation grades, so a single frame tells you which side of
the screen you're on *(observed convention, Digimon Adventure, Toei)*. The world
itself gets a palette law, not just its inhabitants.

## 3. Ed, Edd n Eddy — deliberate colour rule-breaking

### 3.1 The production facts

- The series (a.k.a. Cartoon, Danny Antonucci) was "the last major animated series
  to use traditional cel animation," switching to digital ink-and-paint only in
  2004 — Antonucci wanted production "similar to cartoons from the 1940s to 1970s"
  (Wikipedia, "Ed, Edd n Eddy," Animation section, citing the series' DVD
  materials).
- Its "boiling lines" — shimmering outlines — are made "by tracing off a drawing
  three times through sheets of paper"; Antonucci said the boil "helps keep the
  characters alive" and likened it to 1930s cartoons; IGN compared it to
  Squigglevision, and Animation World Magazine wrote that the varying line gives "a
  distinctive and spontaneous feeling as if it were drawn by children" (Wikipedia,
  ibid.).
- Colour was a *credited design department*, not an afterthought: series credits
  list **Colour Design: Bonni Reid** and Colour Styling (Ginny Ivanicki) alongside
  Art Direction by Antonucci (episode credits, e.g. "Mission Ed-Possible" credits
  as archived on the Super Logos wiki credits pages).
- Even the bodies break rules on purpose: "All the children have multicoloured
  tongues; Antonucci said the idea came after he saw his son and his friends with
  different-coloured tongues because of eating different candy" (Wikipedia, ibid.).
  A mouth interior — the thing every other show standardises — is per-character
  colour-coded.

### 3.2 Why the clashing works: hue chaos on a value leash

The show's colour reputation — garish, off-model, characters clashing against
backgrounds — survives because the chaos is in **hue**, not in **value**. The
cul-de-sac backgrounds sit in a controlled mid-value range of sun-bleached suburban
pastels and greens, so no matter how loud a character's shirt is, figure/ground
separation is carried by value contrast and the heavy boiling ink line. The
jawbreaker — the show's whole economy in one prop — is the palette thesis in
miniature: a giant glossy sphere of swirled, marbled candy colour, desirable
*because* it is all the colours at once, and always rendered with a hard highlight
so it reads at any size. (Production context: the 2000 CN style guide for the
series is described as filled with "unpolished" art where "lines wiggle and
change… giving everything an ethereal sense of false movement," and CBR notes the
show's colours "never faded" but also never took on "the artificial brightness of
later SpongeBob episodes" — a deliberate saturation ceiling held across a decade,
CBR, "This Cartoon Network Classic Sadly Ended an 84-Year Animation Trend.")

**Extracted principle:** you may break hue harmony freely IF (1) value structure is
disciplined, (2) line weight is heavy enough to re-separate figure from ground, and
(3) your saturation ceiling is fixed. Break value too and you get mud. The Eds
break exactly one colour law at a time — and it's never the value law.

## 4. JoJo's Bizarre Adventure — colour as feeling, canon be damned

### 4.1 Araki's stated philosophy: no fixed colours

Araki, asked in an Anime News Network interview (ANN, "Interview: JoJo's Bizarre
Adventure Creator Hirohiko Araki," 2017) why he doesn't stick to a colour scheme
for characters across covers and colour pages: **"I put more emphasis on giving
readers different feelings and impressions through different color combinations."**
The 25th Anniversary long interview records the position as doctrine: "JoJo does
not have a fixed color" (summarised in NamuWiki's Araki entry) — Giorno is
canonically blond in text yet drawn with silver and other hair colours on covers.
Colour is assigned per-image to maximise that image's feeling, full stop.

### 4.2 The secret-weapon pairings: two colours + muted support

From Araki's JoJonium special interview (on Caesar Zeppeli's bandanna): "I like to
play on 'bizarreness' even when I draw in color, so I pay a lot of attention to how
I combine colors with one another. For example — **aqua and pink, blue and white**.
Those combinations are my secret weapons — they give quite an impact even when
looking from a distance… there are many times where I only use **two colors plus a
few more muted ones**." This is the mechanical core under the psychedelic
reputation: a complementary (or near-complementary) **pair** does the impact work;
everything else is deliberately muted so the pair can shout. Impact at distance =
limited palette, not rainbow.

### 4.3 The anime: "special scene coloring" as emotion grammar

David Production's adaptation institutionalised Araki's habit. Director Naokatsu
Tsuda's named addition was **"special scene coloring"** — palette replacement at
moments of emotional significance, "done to pay homage to Araki's own use of
color" (The A.V. Club, "How David Productions finally solved the puzzle of
adaptation"). ANN's art-and-fashion feature (ANN, "Celebrating the Art and Fashion
of JoJo's Bizarre Adventure," 2019) documents the practice: the anime employs
"'color shifts' in the opening songs and during dramatic moments" — Kakyoin shifts
to "a chic black and white look" when he gains the upper hand on Death 13 — and
notes Morioh's surreal look comes from yellow skies and purple hills lifted from
Araki's cover palettes. The rule visible across all examples: **the shift is total
(environment + characters together), sudden (on the story beat, not gradual), and
reversible** (the scene returns to "normal" colour when the emotional moment
passes — which is what makes the shift legible as *grammar* rather than error).

### 4.4 Per-Part identities

Each Part carries its own dominant colour identity in the anime's key art and
grading (Part 1's gothic darks, Part 3's desert golds/blues, Part 4's pastel-
psychedelic Morioh, Part 5's rich Italian golds/pinks) *(observed convention across
David Production's Parts 1–6)* — a season-level colour script: the audience can
identify the Part from a single frame's grade. Colour identity is allowed to be
*serial*: it resets when the story resets, and that reset is itself a signal.

## 5. Colour theory + design principles that apply across all of it

**Harmony systems (Itten, *The Art of Color*, 1961; Albers, *Interaction of
Color*, 1963).** Character ensembles are chord problems. Complementary pairs
(opponents on the wheel) vibrate and read as conflict/energy; analogous runs read
as family/belonging; triadic schemes give three leads equal claim on the eye.
Pokémon's type colours and Araki's pairings are both, functionally, chord choices.
Design ensembles chord-first, individuals second.

**Simultaneous contrast (Chevreul, 1839; Albers, 1963).** A colour is read
relative to its surround: the same gray looks warm on blue, cold on orange. This is
why the Eds can clash and still read (controlled surrounds), and why JoJo shifts
must be *total* — shifting only the character would make the unchanged background
appear to shift anyway, uncontrolled.

**Colour scripting (Pixar method).** Lou Romano, production designer, on the
colour script for *The Incredibles* (MoMA audio guide, "Colorscript. The
Incredibles, 2004"): "It's really just to give… the emotional tone of each scene,
so that you get a sense of not only what the sequences will look like, but what
they should feel like too emotionally, just with pure abstract shapes and color.
You know, the way you might read sheet music." One small painting per beat, whole
film on one sheet, made *before* production because "that is the cheapest moment
to change your mind" (sheets.works' measured study of Romano's 121 *Up* paintings).
*Up*'s script is the textbook case of value/saturation scripting: saturated while
Ellie lives, drained after her death, colour returning with the adventure
(SlashFilm's analysis of Romano's published script). **Saturation is a story
variable; script it like dialogue.**

**Colour temperature as time/mood.** Warm = day, safety, appetite; cool = night,
distance, unease — the convention every source above exploits (Pokémon anime
grades, Digimon's off-world skies, Morioh's wrong-yellow daylight). Temperature
shifts are cheaper than hue shifts and read instantly on small screens.

**Colour as information hierarchy.** The eye goes to highest saturation + highest
value contrast first. Therefore: the most saturated object in frame is the subject
whether you planned it or not. Type colours (Pokémon), attribute colours
(Digimon), and accent disciplines (§Part 2g) are all hierarchy management.

**Limited-palette discipline in TV production.** Cel-era and budget-era TV
animation standardised per-character paint palettes (a fixed, small set of fills
per character) because consistency across hundreds of drawings and multiple
studios demanded it — the Eds' credited Colour Design department (§3.1) is exactly
this job. The discipline outlives the technology: a locked palette per character
is what makes a cast recognisable across episodes, merchandise, and a game.

**Accessibility / small-screen contrast.** Modern delivery is phones. WCAG contrast
guidance (4.5:1 for text) is the floor for subtitles/UI; for characters, the
practical rule inherited from all four sources is *value separation first* (a
character must read in grayscale), colour second. Digimon's redundant shape+colour
coding (§2.1) is the model: never let colour be the only channel carrying meaning.

## (a) Species colourway spec — the Dominant + Accent + Ink structure

Every species colourway is a **four-slot spec**, recorded on its model sheet:

1. **DOMINANT** — the one colour the species is filed under (Pokémon §1.1 rule).
   Covers ≥60% of the body read. One per species, never shared as another lead's
   dominant in the same season cast (see §c).
2. **SUPPORT** — belly/underside/secondary mass. Usually a value-shift of the
   dominant (Dorowani's `#d0f0f0` belly against `#30b0d0` body is the template) or
   a quiet analogous neighbour. Covers most of the rest.
3. **ACCENT** — ≤10% of the read. Eyes, markings, one signature feature
   (Dorowani's red fins; Kittember's gold eyes + tail flame). The accent is the
   species' **silhouette-reading detail in colour form** — it is how you identify
   the species in a crowd frame, in the dark, at thumbnail size.
4. **INK** — `#101010` line and feature ink, shared by the whole cast. Shared ink
   is the family resemblance; it is what makes 47 species one show.

**Assigning a new species' colours (the procedure):**
1. File the dominant first, against the cast grid (§c) — pick the under-used hue
   family for its habitat/temperament slot.
2. Derive the support as a value move (±2 value steps) before reaching for a new
   hue. New hues are spent on accents, not supports.
3. Choose the accent by *complement or deliberate off-note* (Sugimori §1.3): every
   species gets exactly **ONE WRONG NOTE** — one accent or marking that breaks its
   own harmony on purpose (the freckles rule). If the palette is perfectly
   harmonious, it is unfinished.
4. Check the grayscale read: silhouette + accent placement must identify the
   species with colour removed (Eds §3.2: value law is never the one we break).
5. Record all four slots + the wrong note on the model sheet. A species does not
   enter production with an unrecorded palette (Eds' Colour Design department,
   §3.1 — this job exists, and here it is this document).

## (b) Variant colourway law — what LUSTRE may and may never do

Study base: Pokémon shiny practice (§1.4) + Digimon's MetalGreymon (§2.2). The
repo already runs a **lustre** variant colourway per species; this law governs it
and all future variants.

**A variant MAY:**
- Re-palette the DOMINANT and SUPPORT wholesale (hue rotation OR full re-palette —
  both are documented shiny practice; either is legal here, per species, chosen at
  design time by the species' designer, per the Turner rule §1.4).
- Shift value and saturation anywhere (a lustre may be deeper, paler, metallic —
  cf. lustre Dorowani/Mochii running deep-blue against the base sky-blue family).
- Change what the accent is *made of* in rendering (e.g. matte → glow).

**A variant may NEVER:**
- Move, resize, or delete the **accent's placement** (Dorowani's fins stay fins,
  in place, at scale). Shiny practice never touches identifying marks (§1.4).
- Change the **eye colour family** — eyes are identity. (If a variant's eyes must
  read different for story reasons, that's a *form*, §1.5, and needs owner sign-off
  as a form, not a colourway.)
- Change the INK or the ink weight. Ink is the show's, not the species'.
- Re-file the species: the variant does not get its own dominant slot in the cast
  grid (§1.4: shinies have no Pokédex colour of their own). In ensemble frames the
  base form's harmony slot is what counts.
- Exist per-individual: like shinies, a lustre colourway is **one fixed palette
  per species**, identical for every individual of that species (§1.4). Rarity is
  signalled by *staging* (the reveal moment, a sparkle language of our own), never
  by quietly shipping a slightly-off model.

**Allegiance variants:** if a variant ever needs to signal faction/state
(MetalGreymon rule, §2.2), the palette swap must be total-body and paired with a
non-colour channel (a mark, a prop, a sound) — Digimon's redundant-coding rule
(§2.1) applies to us: colour never carries plot alone.

## (c) Ensemble harmony — the pilot six as a chord

The six, by spec:

| Species | Dominant | Support | Accent (wrong note in **bold** type role) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mochii | bright blue `#50b0f0` | pale water blue | clear-drop highlight (**the shine IS the note**) |
| Dorowani | blue `#30b0d0` | cream-pale belly `#d0f0f0`, deep `#3070b0` | accent red `#a03436` fins/eyes (complementary pop) |
| Gabu | teal | sea-green shade | warm shell/belly tone (warm note in a cool body) |
| Kittember | black (ink family) | deep charcoal | gold eyes + **orange tail-tip flame** — flame is ON the tail tip (canon) |
| Sproutle | mint | leaf green helmet mass | blue visor (cool tech note on an organic body) |
| Wispkin | black body | grey smoke | white skull mask (the accent is a *value*, not a hue) |

**How they read as a group:** five of six live in the blue→teal→mint→green
analogous run — the Garden's home chord (water + leaf), which is why brand blue
owns the logo. Against that run, the cast's contrast comes from **value**, not hue:
Kittember and Wispkin are the two black-bodied species (the chord's bass notes),
and the accents supply the complementary sparks in strict ration — Dorowani's red,
Kittember's flame-orange, Gabu's warm tone. It is Araki's structure (§4.2) at cast
scale: one dominant chord + two-colour sparks + muted support. The ensemble is
calm by default so that a red or a flame *means* something when it appears (§g).

**Clash-fix rules (when two leads fight in a scene), in order:**
1. **Value split first.** Separate the two leads by ≥2 value steps (lighten one
   support, deepen the other's shade side). Never fix a clash by changing a hue —
   hues are filed identity (§a).
2. **Accent rationing.** If both leads' accents are warm (Dorowani red + Kittember
   flame in one frame), the *non-speaking* lead's accent is rendered at rest state
   (flame low, fins flat-lit). One hot accent per frame.
3. **Background yields.** The location grade desaturates ~15% behind a two-lead
   shot (§d grades are built with this headroom). Characters never desaturate for
   backgrounds; backgrounds exist to lose.
4. **Third-lead rule.** A third lead entering the frame must come from the opposite
   value pole (a black-body if the frame is light, a light-body if it's dark) or
   enter at reduced scale. Three mid-value leads in one frame is a failed frame —
   recompose, don't recolour.

## (d) Location colour scripts

Each location owns a **dominant family + a temperature policy + a shift rule**.
Grades are per-location, per time-of-day, built as reusable scene grades (Pokémon
anime convention, §1.6; Digimon world-law, §2.5).

| Location | Dominant family | Day policy | Night policy | Shift rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Garden Gate** | brand blues + gate-timber warm brown | high-key, welcoming; the show's "home" grade (Toy Story 3 blue = safety, §5) | lantern-warm pools inside blue dark | never JoJo-shifts; safety is its job |
| **Pond** | water cyan/teal, reflection whites | sparkle highlights, mid saturation | deepest blues in the show; belly-pale `#d0f0f0` becomes moonlight colour | ripples carry grade changes (light moves on water first) |
| **Green** (garden interior) | leaf greens, analogous to cast chord | fullest saturation in the show; food/garden pop (Chowder §f) | firefly accents only; greens go blue-black | saturation is the mood dial here (§e) |
| **Dark Forest** | desaturated blue-green, drained | **daylight that doesn't arrive**: high value, near-zero saturation — bright and wrong | (no night scenes scripted yet — open item) | **JoJo-shift territory (§4.3): the only location licensed for total palette replacement** |

**Time-of-day law:** temperature carries time (warm = morning/safety, neutral =
midday/work, cool = evening/distance, cold-drained = Forest, any hour). A scene may
not change location *and* temperature in the same cut — one colour change per cut,
so the audience always knows which thing changed (simultaneous-contrast discipline,
§5).

## (e) EP01 colour script — "The Garden Has a Ghost"

Format per the Pixar method (§5): one line per beat — dominant / value /
saturation / the one spark. The episode is a mystery that resolves into warmth;
the script drains colour as the mystery deepens and returns it on the reveal.

| Beat | Scene | Dominant | Value | Saturation | The spark |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — First morning | Gate | brand blues | high | full | timber warmth; the home grade, everything in its right colour |
| 2 — Things move | Green | leaf greens | high-mid | full −10% | Kittember's tail flame, low — the only warm note, and it's *looking at something* |
| 3 — Keeper investigates | Green/Pond edge | greens cooling toward teal | mid | −20% | Mochii's bright blue starts reading *cold* instead of cute (temperature does the acting) |
| 4 — The lunge (Gabu misses) | Pond | teal/cyan | mid, contrast UP | full for one beat | the episode's single action-saturation spike; smoke-grey bursts in (Wispkin's support colour, unexplained) |
| 5 — The reveal (Wispkin floats up) | Pond, dusk | deep pond blues | low-mid | −30%, then warming | the white skull mask is the brightest value in the episode so far — dread colour flips to face |
| 6 — Introduction / tag | Gate | brand blues, lantern warmth | mid-high | full, restored +warm | subtitle moment sits inside the warmest frame of the episode (§g) |
| **Stinger** | Dark Forest, DAY | drained blue-green | **high value, ~0 saturation** | **≈0** | none. No spark is the spark. Rule 12 burns in accent red (§g) — the only saturated pixel in the shot |

Script notes: (i) the drain is *value-held* — beats 1–6 keep the Eds' value
discipline (§3.2) while saturation does the storytelling (Up method, §5);
(ii) the stinger breaks the episode's own rule — saturation ≈0 at HIGH value, so
it reads as daylight through a dead filter, wrong in a way night never is;
(iii) Wispkin's mask-white is seeded in beat 4's smoke so the reveal colour has
already appeared once, unexplained (colour-script foreshadowing).

## (f) Pattern & colour — Chowder fills, colour-keyed per location

From the Chowder kit (KIT-INDEX item 1): pattern-fills are locked to the screen
while shapes move through them; the fill is a *compositing* layer, so its colour
is a grade decision, not a paint decision. Rules:

- Each location owns **one pattern family** (Gate: leaf-weave; Pond: ripple rings;
  Green: berry-dot; Forest: **none — pattern never moves in the Forest, and the
  Forest has no fill; that absence is its dread signature**, tying to the
  Environment & Camera standard's inverted-FX rule).
- A creature/property using a pattern-fill takes the fill **in the location's
  colour family, value-matched to its own support slot** — the pattern says
  *where you are*, the silhouette says *who you are*.
- Pattern saturation never exceeds the wearer's dominant saturation. The fill is
  wallpaper, not a second accent (Araki's muted support, §4.2).
- Pattern-fills are banned from accent slots: a pattern may never replace a
  species' accent (flame, fins, mask). Accents stay flat and filed (§a).

## (g) Accent-red discipline + subtitle/UI colour

**The `#a03436` law.** Accent red is the show's scarcest resource and its loudest
signal (§5 hierarchy). It is licensed for exactly:
1. Dorowani's fins/eyes (species accent, §a) — at rest saturation; it is *his*,
   so on him it doesn't count as an alert.
2. **Alerts and wrongness** anywhere else: a red object/prop/light in frame means
   the story is flagging danger or a rule being touched.
3. **The Rule 12 subtitle burn-in** (stinger, §e) — the law made visible.
Any other red in frame is a gate failure (§gate, item 8). Pinks and oranges are
not red and are not restricted — but nothing may sit within Δhue of `#a03436`
except licensed uses, so red never gets diluted by near-red.

**Subtitle colour (silent-film law applied):** creature-meaning subtitles are
designed objects (Persona 5 UI-in-world principle): ink `#101010` text on a
belly-pale `#d0f0f0` card, Fredoka, appearing only at the locked important
moments — EXCEPT Rule-class subtitles (the Manual's Rules), which burn in
**accent red on ink**, no card. The two treatments must never mix within a scene:
a Rule outranks a meaning-subtitle wherever they would collide.

**UI / keeper-tech colour:** keeper devices speak brand blue (screen family
`#70b0d0`–`#b0d0f0`) on ink; status colours on devices follow Digimon's redundant
rule (§2.1) — every status gets colour + shape + label, never colour alone.
Warning states on devices use flame-orange, NOT accent red: red is story, orange
is machinery. WCAG 4.5:1 contrast minimum for all text over picture (§5).

## COLOUR GATE — per-asset checklist (run before any asset ships)

1. ☐ Species spec recorded: dominant / support / accent / ink slots + the ONE
     WRONG NOTE, on the model sheet. (§a)
2. ☐ Grayscale test passes: species identifiable by silhouette + accent
     placement with colour removed. (§a, §3.2)
3. ☐ Variant check: if lustre/variant — accent placement, eye colour family, and
     ink unchanged; palette fixed per species, not per individual. (§b)
4. ☐ Ensemble check: no two leads in frame share a dominant family AND a value
     band; clash fixes applied in order (value → accent ration → BG yield). (§c)
5. ☐ One hot accent per frame, maximum. (§c)
6. ☐ Location grade matches the location script (family + temperature policy);
     one colour change per cut. (§d)
7. ☐ Episode position check: saturation/value match the episode colour script
     for this beat. (§e)
8. ☐ Red audit: every red/near-red in frame is licensed (Dorowani, alert, Rule).
     (§g)
9. ☐ Pattern audit: fills are location-keyed, value-matched, below dominant
     saturation, never in an accent slot; Forest frames have NO pattern. (§f)
10. ☐ Text/UI audit: subtitle treatment correct (meaning-card vs Rule burn-in,
     never mixed in a scene); device statuses triple-coded; text contrast ≥4.5:1.
     (§g)
